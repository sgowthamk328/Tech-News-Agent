## Japan's Fugaku supercomputer has decidedly un-super security

**Category:** Policy / Security  |  **Found via:** The Register

Japan's flagship supercomputer, Fugaku, has been taken offline following a serious cybersecurity breach at the RIKEN Center for Computational Science. The incident began between November 10 and 13, when unknown attackers compromised an operational status dashboard. From that initial foothold, the intruders traversed RIKEN's internal network, installed malware, and accessed sensitive servers holding user credentials and contact information. Notably, these compromised credentials included access keys for external supercomputing clusters operated by other institutions, which the attackers subsequently attempted to use. When an unauthorized login attempt directly targeting Fugaku was detected on November 13, administrators quickly pulled the massive machine off the network to prevent malicious workloads from executing across its 7.6 million processor cores.

This incident directly impacts more than 4,200 registered researchers using Fugaku, as well as nearly 400 users of the smaller Rikyu system. Because supercomputers run tightly controlled, advance-booked schedules for intensive scientific experiments, halting Fugaku causes immediate logistical backlogs across domestic and international research projects. While RIKEN reports no confirmed evidence that proprietary research data was stolen, the incident exposes how secondary utility assets—like a public-facing monitoring dashboard—can serve as lateral stepping stones into high-value national infrastructure.

Moving forward, RIKEN is conducting a deep forensic probe alongside external institutions whose systems were linked to the exposed credentials. The attack adds pressure to Japanese authorities, who recently approved emergency funding to bolster national cyber defenses following high-profile breaches across domestic telecommunications, cloud hosting, and retail operations.

Who's involved: RIKEN Center for Computational Science, the Fugaku supercomputer, and the Rikyu supercomputer.

## 350 tech jobs hammered as TCS takes over work at iconic Australian retailer

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** The Register

Major Australian hardware retailer Bunnings Warehouse has initiated an aggressive restructuring of its technology division, eliminating roughly one-third of its internal IT workforce to transition duties to Indian IT services multinational Tata Consultancy Services (TCS). The move cuts 230 permanent staff and terminates 120 contractor roles, leaving Bunnings with an in-house tech staff of approximately 500 employees. The transition will shift several technical functions offshore to India and the Philippines, alongside some onshore TCS personnel, with the restructured operating model slated to fully commence in July 2027.

This development illustrates a broader macro trend across enterprise IT, where legacy consumer brands are turning to multinational systems integrators and offshore delivery centers to rein in operational costs while reorienting investments toward artificial intelligence and customer-facing digital applications. Bunnings explicitly defended the partnership by arguing that modernized retail requires a fast-moving, scalable technology partner capable of sustaining initiatives like its newly launched AI-powered customer concierge, which leverages Google Cloud's Gemini Enterprise foundation models.

For the Australian tech market, this announcement delivers another blow to local engineering employment, arriving on the heels of comparable downsizings at major domestic employers such as Canva, Atlassian, and national carrier Qantas, which recently offshored technical roles to Accenture. Beyond the labor impact, the transition poses operational risks for Bunnings. The retailer maintains a unique cultural reputation in Australia rooted in community goodwill and in-person store advice, sparking internal skepticism about whether offshore contract personnel can effectively support the bespoke nuances of the brand's operational culture.

Who's involved: Bunnings Warehouse, Tata Consultancy Services (TCS), and Google (Gemini Enterprise platform).

## US extends chip sale restrictions to more Asian countries to reduce risk of ‘diversion’ to China

**Category:** Policy / Security  |  **Found via:** The Register

The United States government has significantly expanded its export control regime to prevent China from accessing cutting-edge artificial intelligence computing power. Previously, the U.S. focused primarily on preventing the direct sale and shipment of advanced semiconductors to Chinese organizations. However, international tech companies and foreign cloud providers quickly created a workaround: instead of importing the physical silicon, Chinese entities simply rented access to powerful graphics processing units (GPUs) located in third-party data centers abroad. To close this loophole, the U.S. Department of Commerce’s Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) issued an interim final rule that places restrictions on data centers operating across Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil.

Under these new rules, operators in these five jurisdictions are prohibited from offering remote access to certain high-performance GPUs and servers without authorization. Furthermore, vendors must now secure specific U.S. export licenses before shipping advanced AI hardware into these nations. The policy shifts regulatory responsibility directly onto cloud operators, introducing stringent 'Know Your Customer's Workload' compliance mandates akin to anti-money laundering checks in banking. Providers must rigorously verify who is renting their infrastructure and demonstrate to American authorities that their computing clusters are not being routed back into embargoed jurisdictions. While workloads powering standard commercial recommender systems (such as online retail algorithms) are exempt, core AI training and inference infrastructures are strictly covered.

Going forward, data center hubs in Southeast Asia face substantial operational friction and risk losing access to American chips if they fail to monitor their tenants effectively. In the short term, this imposes severe computing bottlenecks on Chinese AI labs. In the long term, industry analysts anticipate that this pressure will further accelerate geopolitical decoupling, pushing Chinese tech firms to invest heavily in domestic chip design and sovereign hardware ecosystems.

Who's involved: U.S. Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS), Counterpoint Research, and data center operators across Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, Indonesia, and Brazil.

## Hacker behind 2022 GTA VI leaks pleads guilty

**Category:** Policy / Security  |  **Found via:** The Verge

Arion Kurtaj, a core member of the notorious international cybercrime syndicate known as Lapsus$, has formally pleaded guilty to a slate of high-profile cyber intrusions, notably including the massive September 2022 breach of video game publisher Rockstar Games. During that breach, Kurtaj exfiltrated and leaked roughly 90 confidential in-development gameplay videos of the highly anticipated title Grand Theft Auto VI. Kurtaj's legal saga represents a significant milestone in how international law enforcement and judicial systems handle persistent, highly capable juvenile cybercriminals operating across borders.

The criminal timeline shows that Kurtaj initially faced charges in late 2021 for unauthorized SIM-swapping attacks against telecommunications provider BT/EE. While out on bail and residing in a hotel under protective supervision in September 2022, he managed to bypass restrictions by utilizing a mobile phone, an Amazon Fire Stick, and the hotel room's television internet connection to orchestrate intrusions into major corporations, including Uber, Revolut, and Rockstar Games. The legal proceedings were complicated by health assessments; Kurtaj was originally ordered into a secure hospital indefinitely in late 2023 due to acute autism, but medical evaluations in early 2024 subsequently deemed him mentally fit to stand trial, resulting in his transfer to a standard prison facility and his ultimate guilty plea.

What happens next is formal sentencing, scheduled to take place before a UK court on November 23rd. The case serves as a major case study in digital forensics and corporate infrastructure defense, exposing how even modest consumer hardware can be leveraged to infiltrate Fortune 500 networks through social engineering, SIM-swapping, and credential abuse. For the broader tech and entertainment industries, the plea closes one of the most visible corporate intellectual property breaches of the decade.

Who's involved: Arion Kurtaj, the Lapsus$ hacking collective, the UK Crown Prosecution Service (CPS), and targeted companies including Rockstar Games, Uber, Revolut, and BT/EE.

## Robo-soldier startup backed by Eric Trump misses payroll, may collapse

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** Ars Technica

Humanoid robotics startup Foundation Future Industries faces potential collapse after failing to meet its late October payroll, highlighting the perils of hype-driven fundraising in early-stage deep tech. Founded with ambitious promises of deploying combat-ready bipedal robots, the company sought a $3 billion valuation on the back of claims that its machines were actively testing in war zones like Ukraine and Gaza. However, those claims rapidly unraveled: investigative accounts revealed that delivery attempts were turned back at national borders, Israeli defense officials refused to corroborate testing talks, and demonstrations amounted to rudimentary staged motions rather than combat autonomy. Compounding these technical realities are corporate governance scandals, including reports that CEO Sankaet Pathak borrowed $4.6 million from company coffers for personal real estate.

The startup's plight underscores the vast gap between investor narratives and the practical reality of robotics engineering. Building general-purpose humanoid robots capable of navigating unpredictable physical environments demands immense capital, advanced sensor integration, and years of iterative software design. While commercial applications are only beginning trial runs in structured environments like warehouses, military applications require even higher reliability. The defense sector has instead found practical success with unmanned ground vehicles running on treads and wheels—proven, mechanically simpler platforms that perform logistical runs, mine-clearing, and strike missions. Foundation's struggle illustrates the broader industry danger of relying on high-profile political backers and exaggerated battlefield capabilities rather than demonstrable engineering milestones to sustain cash-intensive research and development. When capital markets tighten, companies that prioritize media theater over validated hardware-software integration quickly exhaust their runway.

Who's involved: Foundation Future Industries, CEO Sankaet Pathak, President Mike LeBlanc, and strategic advisor Eric Trump.

## United Airlines system outage disrupts departures just days after the big ground stop

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** The Verge

United Airlines suffered a systemwide technology disruption that temporarily halted departing flights across its network, demonstrating how modern commercial aviation depends entirely on centralized digital infrastructure. Modern flight operations rely on a chain of specialized software tools that automate everything from flight planning to passenger manifests. In this incident, passengers and flight crews reported that the failure centered on the carrier's automated weight and balance calculator. Before any commercial aircraft can legally take off, dispatchers and pilots must calculate the distribution of passengers, cargo, and fuel to ensure the aircraft's center of gravity remains within strict safety limits dictated by aviation authorities. When the software running these real-time calculations crashes, flight crews cannot receive official dispatch clearance to push back from the gate or initiate takeoffs.

Although United quickly resolved the underlying software glitch and confirmed that airborne flights and regional United Express operations were unaffected, the disruption reflects a broader industry vulnerability. Commercial airlines operate on razor-thin scheduling margins, meaning even brief software failures trigger ripple effects across gate availability, crew rest requirements, and connecting itineraries. The breakdown arrived during a week already plagued by critical infrastructure disruptions, including an FAA ground stop caused by air traffic control hardware problems. Because airlines maintain sprawling legacy architectures patched together with newer cloud services, a single faulty endpoint or corrupted database can freeze critical dispatch operations. For the transportation sector, this event reinforces that operational resilience depends as much on software reliability, redundant systems, and rapid fallback workflows as it does on mechanical maintenance.

Who's involved: United Airlines, flight crews, and impacted passengers.
