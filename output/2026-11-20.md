## Support tech fixed malware by charging client less for their services

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** The Register

This story comes from The Register’s long-running human-interest tech support series, "On Call," which highlights real-world experiences from IT professionals. In this account, an independent IT consultant—anonymized as "Dennis"—recounts an episode from his career serving a prominent lawyer in a major U.S. city. For years, Dennis earned regular monthly revenue by manually cleaning malware from his client’s Windows PC, a problem driven largely by the client’s unsafe web browsing habits.

Eventually, Dennis prioritized his client’s operational reliability over his own recurring revenue. He advised the attorney to replace the Windows machine with an Apple iMac, explicitly forecasting that the transition would eliminate frequent malware infections and save money long term. Despite the client’s initial concern that Dennis would lose income, Dennis insisted on giving the best technical advice, sacrificing an estimated $6,000 or more in ongoing support fees over subsequent years.

The hardware change dramatically reduced security incidents; the client encountered a potential threat on macOS only once over a five-year period, which Dennis easily resolved with a standard operating system reinstallation. The narrative underscores a fundamental industry dynamic: the tension between billable support hours and proactive solution design that prioritizes customer outcomes over short-term revenue.

Who's involved: IT consultant "Dennis", an unnamed attorney client, Apple (iMac), and The Register (publication).

## Big Tech's compliance with EU market rules is performative, consumer group says

**Category:** Policy / Security  |  **Found via:** The Register

The European Consumer Organisation (BEUC), an umbrella group representing consumer groups across Europe, has filed a comprehensive analysis with the European Commission alleging that major technology companies are failing to genuinely comply with the EU’s landmark Digital Markets Act (DMA). Enacted to curb anti-competitive behavior, the DMA designates major tech platforms—specifically Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, ByteDance, Meta, and Microsoft—as official "gatekeepers" subject to strict rules designed to ensure open competition and consumer choice.

BEUC’s report, which aggregates evidence collected between mid-2025 and mid-2026, accuses these gatekeepers of engaging in "cosmetic compliance." According to the findings, companies continue to employ deceptive user interface design patterns—often referred to as "dark patterns"—and non-neutral wording to nudge users into consenting to cross-service data sharing. Additionally, the report outlines persistent barriers preventing consumers from easily altering default software, uninstalling pre-installed applications, or switching to competing web browsers, search engines, and app marketplaces.

For users and businesses in the EU, the filing puts pressure on regulators to move beyond policy setting and enforce strict compliance. If the European Commission acts on BEUC's recommendations, gatekeepers could face formal investigations and substantial monetary fines—which can reach up to 10 percent of a company's global annual revenue—forcing Big Tech to redesign their operating systems, app stores, and consent workflows across the European market.

Who's involved: European Consumer Organisation (BEUC), European Commission (EC), Alphabet (Google), Amazon, Apple, ByteDance, Meta, and Microsoft.

**Source:** https://www.theregister.com/legal/2026/11/20/big-techs-compliance-with-eu-market-rules-is-performative-consumer-group-says/5311066

## US let Nvidia and AMD sell bucketloads of chips to military-linked Chinese companies

**Category:** Policy / Security  |  **Found via:** The Register

United States export control enforcement has come under intense congressional scrutiny following disclosures regarding semiconductor licensing to China. Representative Brian Mast, chair of the House Foreign Affairs Committee, revealed records from the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security showing that federal regulators approved export licenses allowing Nvidia and AMD to ship hundreds of thousands of advanced graphics processing units to Chinese tech giants, including Alibaba and Tencent. These companies appear on the Department of Defense's list of Chinese military companies under military-civil fusion designations, which identify civilian commercial entities that actively assist China's defense industrial base.

The regulatory mechanisms governing high-performance computing are designed specifically to prevent military adversaries from acquiring processors capable of accelerating artificial intelligence model training, aerodynamic simulation, and weapons design. Under these export policies, vendors must obtain explicit government waivers verifying that transfers pose a low risk of diversion to military end-users. Despite denying large batch requests, the Bureau of Industry and Security granted permits for 879,000 components out of 3.4 million requested units. Scrutiny has escalated over allegations that some applications involved intermediate front entities intended to route hardware through third parties.

In response to enforcement vulnerabilities, Washington introduced revised restrictions barring domestic providers from granting remote access to advanced computing infrastructure. While Chinese cloud hyperscalers report immediate friction and reductions in near-term data center expansion targets, domestic semiconductor initiatives in China aim to establish self-sufficient domestic production lines before the end of the decade. As congressional leaders demand stricter oversight, regulators face pressure to tighten approval criteria and close offshore diversion loopholes, while commercial chip vendors risk permanently losing access to Chinese buyers as local alternative hardware matures.

Who's involved: U.S. Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS), Representative Brian Mast, Nvidia, AMD, Alibaba, and Tencent.

In this historic offering, artificial intelligence lab Anthropic debuted on Nasdaq, setting records by raising over $81 billion through standard stock sales alongside an additional $26 billion via non-voting Class N shares sold directly to strategic tech partners. The core business logic driving such an unprecedented public debut lies in the immense capital requirements needed to build, train, and deploy frontier artificial intelligence models. Training state-of-the-art AI systems requires massive compute clusters, creating long-term server and infrastructure commitments estimated to top $1 trillion over the coming years.

By executing an IPO, Anthropic transitions its fundraising foundation from private venture capital rounds to public equity markets and institutional corporate debt. The deal structure allowed primary cloud infrastructure partners Amazon and Google to solidify major equity positions, together controlling nearly 28% of the company by converting prior convertible notes and strategic cash infusions into formal equity. Concurrently, a syndicate of hardware and cloud providers including Nvidia, AMD, Microsoft, Broadcom, Micron, and TSMC purchased specialized non-voting shares. This private placement effectively ties key hardware vendors to Anthropic's long-term supply chain. Meanwhile, Anthropic's seven co-founders collectively cashed out nearly $2 billion by selling a minor fraction of their shares, while pledging 80% of their remaining personal equity to philanthropic causes.

This transaction signals a broader maturation of the AI industry. It demonstrates that public markets possess strong appetite for mega-scale AI companies despite substantial ongoing net operational losses. It also marks a pivotal shift away from early circular financing arrangements toward transparent, market-driven capital structures capable of bankrolling multi-hundred-billion-dollar data center commitments.

## Anthropic rival says companies can (mostly) skip the pricey models

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** TechCrunch

The artificial intelligence industry is witnessing an accelerating debate over model architecture, cost efficiency, and enterprise data control. At the center of this landscape are two distinct approaches to AI deployment: closed, proprietary frontier models hosted by large tech conglomerates, and open-weight models that businesses can customize, host, and run independently. While frontier labs like Anthropic and OpenAI offer unmatched generalized intelligence, their API token fees can become prohibitively expensive for high-volume corporate applications. In response, specialized intelligence platforms such as Fireworks AI are making the case that many enterprise tasks can be handled more economically using open-source alternatives.

The contextual driver behind this market shift is the narrowing capability gap between open-weight models and closed systems. Many enterprises are transitioning toward a dual-model framework, often called intelligent routing. Under this operational model, complex planning and multi-step reasoning are assigned to top-tier proprietary systems, whereas repetitive tasks, domain-specific querying, and basic arithmetic are delegated to smaller, fine-tuned open models. Beyond cost savings, this approach addresses corporate governance concerns regarding data privacy and the potential leakage of proprietary know-how to external model developers.

However, adopting open-weight infrastructure introduces distinct operational and cybersecurity challenges. Hosting specialized models requires managing API keys, data storage pipelines, and secure cloud environments. Recent security incidents involving credential theft and exposed customer datasets at hosting providers underscore the delicate balance startups face between offering rapid customization and ensuring enterprise-grade protection. For the broader tech industry, these dynamics signal that corporate AI adoption is moving past initial experimentation into a mature phase focused on unit economics, data sovereignty, security compliance, and vendor diversification. As competition intensifies, both open-source hosts and frontier labs must continuously prove their value propositions to enterprise software buyers.

Who's involved: Fireworks AI (co-founded by Chenyu Zhao), Anthropic, OpenAI, Microsoft, Uber, Notion, Palo Alto Networks, and Nvidia.

## Amazon fights the Delivery Protection Act with homepage banner, in-app pop-up

**Category:** Policy / Security  |  **Found via:** Ars Technica

Amazon has begun using its primary consumer platform—including its website homepage, checkout confirmation screens, and mobile application pop-ups—to actively campaign against local municipal legislation in New York City. The targeted legislation, known as the Delivery Protection Act (Introduction 0518-2026), would mandate that last-mile delivery providers directly employ their delivery drivers rather than relying on third-party contractor networks. Amazon's digital messaging warns local consumers that the proposed ordinance could force the company to alter its local logistics network, potentially increasing delivery costs by up to $5 per order and slowing down fulfillment speeds within the city.

This policy struggle directly impacts New York City residents, independent delivery service partners, delivery gig workers, and commercial logistics firms operating within the municipality, including competitors like FedEx and DoorDash. If enacted, the law would restructure the employment model for urban logistics operations across the city, shifting liability and benefit obligations directly onto parent platform corporations. The bill is currently pending before the New York City Council's Committee on Consumer and Worker Protection, where it awaits potential advancing action.

This campaign establishes a significant precedent in corporate digital advocacy, marking a shift where major e-commerce platforms leverage real-time access to consumer interfaces to influence political outcomes. By deploying in-app alerts and direct action links, Amazon is adopting public advocacy tactics previously used by ride-sharing platforms to mobilize consumer sentiment against regulatory changes. The strategy highlights growing friction between municipal labor regulations and the contractor-based logistics models that support rapid e-commerce delivery networks.

Who's involved: Amazon, the New York City Council, Mayor Zohran Mamdani, and the International Brotherhood of Teamsters.

In its third-quarter financial results, the company disclosed revenue of $115.8 billion, representing a doubling of sales compared to the previous year. This revenue surge was propelled by strong global demand for its data center processors and the rapid deployment of its new Vera Rubin hardware architecture. However, despite these operational achievements, Nvidia's share price plummeted nearly 40 percent from its peak, reaching new multi-month lows as non-financial crises destabilized investor confidence.

The volatility stems primarily from a sudden leadership crisis and regulatory oversight. Longtime Chief Executive Officer Jensen Huang resigned following personal conduct investigations and news reports alleging connections to a target of foreign espionage. The market's sharp reaction reflects how closely Nvidia's corporate identity and market dominance were associated with its founder. The uncertainty is compounded by macroeconomic concerns regarding potential overinvestment in AI infrastructure, potential legislative pushbacks against regional data center expansion following midterm election shifts, and scheduled congressional testimony regarding security protocols and overseas operational links.

For the broader technology sector, this turbulence highlights the fragile balance between high-performing hardware delivery and key-person risk in fast-growing industries. Nvidia supplies the essential computing foundation for major artificial intelligence developers, cloud services providers, and enterprise operations worldwide. A prolonged leadership vacuum or heightened regulatory scrutiny could delay long-term product roadmaps, affect supply chain allocation agreements, or shift competitive dynamics toward rival semiconductor manufacturers and newly listed AI companies competing for capital.

## MAHA PAC spent more on steak, luxury club than candidates, report shows

**Category:** Policy / Security  |  **Found via:** Ars Technica

A financial disclosure analysis of the Make America Healthy Again (MAHA) Political Action Committee revealed that the organization allocated the vast majority of its capital to executive overhead, media production, high-end entertainment, and inter-organizational transfers rather than direct candidate campaign support during the recent midterm election cycle. Political action committees (PACs) generally function to aggregate financial contributions to fund independent political advertisements, voter outreach, and direct candidate advocacy. However, public campaign filings analyzed in recent investigative reporting showed that out of more than $10.5 million disbursed by the MAHA PAC, less than ten percent—approximately $1 million—was directed toward independent expenditures favoring political candidates.

Instead of funding political campaign ads, significant sums were directed toward private social clubs, upscale dining, executive security arrangements, media production firms, political influencers, and credit card payments. Furthermore, over $3 million was transferred directly to an affiliated non-profit entity, MAHA Action, where specific outlay details remain unitemized under standard campaign disclosure requirements. This financial allocation pattern coincided with severe midterm election losses for candidates endorsed by the movement across multiple high-profile national and regional congressional races.

The findings affect campaign donors, political strategists, public health policy advocates, and candidates who relied on the PAC's promised financial backing. The stark contrast between initial fundraising projections and actual spending on campaign activities has drawn scrutiny from campaign finance researchers, who note that major political action committees typically reserve the vast majority of their budgets for direct political messaging. Moving forward, the disclosure is expected to intensify debates regarding campaign finance transparency, the oversight of political action committee expenditures, and the operational accountability of non-profit advocacy networks engaged in electoral politics.

Who's involved: MAHA PAC, PAC leader Tony Lyons, Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., researcher Andrew Mayersohn (OpenSecrets), and candidate Richard Pan.
