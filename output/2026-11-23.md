## Techie turned his too-easy job interview into a trap

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** The Register

In computer hardware maintenance, troubleshooting physical components and configuration settings is a foundational skill. Older desktop computer power supply units (PSUs) frequently included a manual red toggle switch on the back to select between different regional input voltages—typically 110V (standard in North America) and 230V (standard in the UK and Europe). If a PC configured for 110V is plugged into a 230V wall socket, the overvoltage will rapidly cause the power supply components to fail, often resulting in a loud pop, smoke, and permanent hardware destruction.

This lighthearted workplace anecdote from The Register's recurring 'Who, Me?' column explores the culture of technical interviews and practical competency assessments in IT operations. In tech support and systems administration hiring, employers often set up 'broken' test rigs with deliberate faults—such as disconnected internal cables, unseated expansion cards, or disabled BIOS settings—to evaluate a candidate's diagnostic process and attention to detail. 

The anecdote highlights how interview testing standards can vary significantly between grassroots technicians and management. The job applicant, pseudonymously named 'Marshall,' passed a practical hardware assessment by fixing basic internal connections. Believing the evaluation was overly simplistic, he suggested toggling the PSU voltage switch to test whether future candidates checked hardware safety parameters before applying power. Although his manager dismissed the idea as too harsh, a subsequent incident occurred months later where an individual plugged in a misconfigured power supply and destroyed the unit. 

While presented as an amusing historical footnote from two decades ago, the context underscores broader operational dynamics in IT support: balancing basic troubleshooting procedures against hardware safety risks, and the ongoing challenge of designing fair, realistic technical skill evaluations without exposing lab equipment to destructive errors.

Who's involved: The Register, an anonymous IT professional ('Marshall'), and an unnamed technology employer.

## Google claims last week’s critical VMware bug was under attack in September

**Category:** Policy / Security  |  **Found via:** The Register

Threat intelligence researchers at Google's Mandiant group revealed that a critical zero-day vulnerability in VMware vCenter, designated CVE-2026-123890, was actively exploited months before public disclosure. VMware vCenter serves as the centralized management console for VMware vSphere environments, controlling host servers and guest virtual machines across enterprise data centers. Because vCenter holds high-level administrative access across entire virtualized infrastructures, compromising it gives attackers unrestricted access to sensitive virtual servers and enterprise storage volumes.

According to security reports, a sophisticated China-linked threat actor designated ARMORY CASTLE exploited the flaw in September after gaining initial access through compromised perimeter remote-access appliances. Once inside the victim's network, the attackers deployed a specialized suite of custom malware tools, notably a tool called 'BONESAW.' This tool allowed the attackers to forge authentication tokens, bypass multi-factor authentication (MFA), and impersonate administrative accounts. The adversary leveraged this access to map the infrastructure, gain root-level access to underlying ESXi hypervisors, and steal data directly from Virtual Machine File System (VMFS) volumes.

This incident directly affects enterprise organizations running unpatched versions of VMware vCenter, particularly those managing high-value virtualized assets. What happens next is an urgent operational pivot toward patch deployment and defensive hardening. VMware released security patches for the vulnerability on November 19 and advised all administrators to apply them immediately under the assumption that active exploitation will continue. 

Additionally, the incident highlights a ongoing policy controversy regarding security updates. Following Broadcom's acquisition of VMware, free public patch downloads for perpetual license holders without active support contracts were restricted. Although Broadcom confirmed its policy to provide zero-day critical security fixes for perpetual vSphere 8 users through October 2027, affected customers must now proactively contact support to obtain necessary security binaries, raising concerns about patch accessibility for legacy installations.

Who's involved: Google Mandiant, VMware (Broadcom), BeyondTrust, and state-sponsored threat group ARMORY CASTLE.

## Kodak’s next collectible is a tiny digital photo frame that doubles as a tracker

**Category:** Technical Release  |  **Found via:** The Verge

The Kodak SlideTag, manufactured by Reto, is a tiny keychain accessory that combines a digital photo display with Bluetooth item-tracking technology. Styled after classic film slides, the device solves the problem of single-use, non-functional novelties by combining digital personalization with practical utility. At its core, the device features a 1.54-inch color E Ink screen with a resolution of 240 x 240 pixels. Unlike traditional LCD or OLED displays that require continuous power to emit light, E Ink screens use electrophoretic technology—in this case, mixing six colored ink pigments—to display static images. Because E Ink only consumes energy when the image refreshes, the device achieves significant power efficiency, enabling it to run on a standard, replaceable CR2032 coin cell battery.

To transfer images, custom text, to-do lists, or QR codes to the device, users connect their smartphone via Bluetooth using Kodak’s Memory Link application. The unit can store and cycle through up to 12 images locally. For its tracking functionality, the SlideTag integrates with major crowdsourced location networks, specifically Apple’s Find My and Google’s Find Hub. When a user loses an item attached to the tag, nearby third-party devices on these networks anonymously detect the SlideTag's Bluetooth beacon and relay its location to the owner. While it lacks advanced spatial-ranging technologies like Ultra-Wideband (UWB) or Channel Sounding found in premium trackers, it includes a built-in 75-decibel speaker that can be triggered remotely within a 30-meter Bluetooth range to help users locate their belongings by sound.

Who's involved: Kodak, Reto, Apple (Find My network), and Google (Find Hub network).

## $1 of GPT-5.1 tokens finds new ways around China’s Great Firewall

**Category:** Research  |  **Found via:** The Register

Researchers from UMass Amherst, UC Boulder, and the Great Firewall Report developed an automated system named CircumVolve that leverages large language models (LLMs) like GPT-5.1 to discover strategies for evading internet censorship. Traditionally, bypassing advanced filtering systems like China's Great Firewall requires manual protocol analysis, custom script writing, and extensive trial-and-error testing. CircumVolve automates this process by combining LLM-driven Python code generation with real-time, feedback-driven evaluation across the network stack. It models censorship techniques as discrete scenarios and generates code to manipulate packet headers, exploit DNS parser ambiguities, or alter encrypted QUIC packets. The system achieved successful evasions against simulated and real-world censorship nodes in China, Iran, Pakistan, and Kazakhstan at a median API token cost of less than $1. This research is novel because it demonstrates that AI agents can dramatically lower the technical and financial barriers to discovering censorship circumvention methods, empowering under-resourced digital freedom advocates to keep pace with state-level censors.

Who's involved: UMass Amherst, University of Colorado Boulder, Great Firewall Report, and OpenAI (GPT-5.1).

## OpenAI’s chief scientist has thoughts on how to limit AI development

**Category:** Policy / Security  |  **Found via:** TechCrunch

This story details a proposal from OpenAI Chief Scientist Jakub Pachocki regarding how the artificial intelligence industry could establish formal safety speed limits on frontier AI model development. As AI systems become more powerful, public officials, safety researchers, and tech executives have debated how to pace development to prevent catastrophic risks. However, enforcing safety regulations requires a standardized, reliable metric to determine how rapidly models are advancing. Traditional benchmarks that test specific tasks—such as taking standardized tests or coding—often become obsolete quickly as models surpass human capabilities on those tests.

Pachocki proposes using "perplexity" as a universal standard to measure AI growth. In machine learning, perplexity measures how accurately a model predicts the next word or token in a sequence; a lower perplexity score means the model makes better predictions and possesses a stronger statistical understanding of information. OpenAI suggests that AI companies could agree to safety commitments anchored to perplexity thresholds. Under this framework, developers would voluntarily pause or slow model training once perplexity falls below a specific numeric level until concrete safety, monitoring, and alignment milestones are achieved.

If adopted across the industry, this proposal would affect major AI labs, safety regulators, and policymakers seeking standard metrics for AI governance. It moves the regulatory conversation away from broad, qualitative safety promises toward measurable statistical benchmarks. Next, frontier AI labs like OpenAI and Anthropic, alongside international safety institutes, will evaluate whether technical metrics like perplexity can serve as a binding basis for voluntary industry standards or future government policies.

Who's involved: OpenAI, Jakub Pachocki, Sam Altman, Anthropic, Dario Amodei, and Elon Musk.

## GLP-1 drugs could have a surprisingly large environmental payoff

**Category:** Research  |  **Found via:** Ars Technica

Researchers published a novel simulation study evaluating how widespread adoption of GLP-1 weight-loss medications (such as Ozempic and Wegovy) could reshape global food systems and land use. GLP-1 drugs work physiologically by mimicking a hormone that targets brain regions regulating appetite, leading to lower calorie intake among patients. The researchers used a complex computational economic model to project the macro-level systemic consequences if broad populations of adults living with obesity reduce their overall food consumption. 

The novelty of this study lies in connecting individual pharmaceutical treatments to global agricultural economics and environmental outcomes. Traditionally, weight-loss drugs are analyzed strictly through clinical health metrics like body mass index or cardiovascular risks. This research broadens the lens to model downstream economic feedback loops: reduced aggregate demand for calories decreases the total demand placed on agricultural supply chains. The model estimates that widespread global access to GLP-1 medications could free up 32 million to 46 million hectares of farmland by 2050 (especially when paired with dietary shifts toward healthier choices) while preventing millions of premature deaths annually. 

However, the study also reveals economic complexities. Lower food demand in wealthy regions can depress global food prices, which might inadvertently spur higher food consumption among non-medicated populations. Moving forward, these insights enable agricultural policymakers and climate researchers to model how medical interventions interact with environmental policy, showing that public health treatments can serve as unexpected drivers of ecological and land-use changes.

Who's involved: Roslyn Henry (University of Aberdeen), Peter Alexander (University of Edinburgh), and Nature Communications (publishing journal).

**Source:** https://arstechnica.com/health/2026/11/glp-1-drugs-could-have-a-surprisingly-large-environmental-payoff/

*The exact projected figures (saving up to 7 million lives annually and freeing up 46 million hectares of land by 2050) are model-based estimates rather than empirical facts.*
