## Signal restricts mobile screenshots as it confronts ‘existential threat’ of AI surveillance

**Category:** Policy / Security  |  **Found via:** TechCrunch

Secure messaging service Signal has updated its mobile applications on Android and iOS to restrict the ability to take screenshots within the app. This change builds on a similar measure previously introduced in its desktop application for Windows. The decision comes in direct response to the rapid rise of system-level artificial intelligence tools—often referred to as agentic AI—built directly into modern mobile and desktop operating systems. Operating systems from major providers now increasingly feature background AI assistants designed to continuously capture, analyze, and store on-screen information to assist users with automated tasks. Signal’s leadership views this pervasive screen scanning as a fundamental threat to end-to-end encryption, because once sensitive message text is displayed on a user's screen and scanned by an operating system's AI agent, that data is extracted into external local databases or remote cloud servers outside of Signal's encrypted pipeline.

This policy update directly impacts millions of Signal users who rely on the platform for confidential communication. By restricting native screen capture capabilities on mobile devices, Signal creates a protective barrier preventing OS-level background agents from grabbing visual data from active chat windows. However, recognizing user autonomy, Signal allows individuals to manually opt out and disable the screenshot blocking setting in their app options. Nevertheless, Signal warns that turning off this safeguard breaks the application's independent privacy guarantees. The company also candidly acknowledges that restricting screenshots is an incomplete defense, as operating system providers may develop deeper system integrations or alternative data capture methods that bypass surface-level UI restrictions.

Moving forward, this move highlights an escalating conflict between consumer privacy software and operating system developers pushing deeply integrated AI surveillance capabilities. As mobile OS platforms double down on continuous screen context processing, privacy-focused developers will need to implement increasingly stringent technical controls to preserve message confidentiality on compromised user hardware.

Who's involved: Signal Foundation, Signal President Meredith Whittaker, Apple (iOS), and Google (Android).

## “Race realism” isn’t realistic—and it isn’t good science

**Category:** Research  |  **Found via:** Ars Technica

This article examines the scientific claims underlying 'race realism' or 'hereditarianism'—the belief that genetic differences primarily drive variations in intelligence and behavior between racial groups. Modern biological and behavioral research refutes these claims through multiple lines of evidence. In human genetics, foundational work by Richard Lewontin and subsequent human genome mapping demonstrate that genetic variation within any single self-identified racial group far exceeds the variation between different racial groups. Ancient DNA studies further show continuous historical migration and intermixing, invalidating concepts of genetic purity. Social science research further undermines hereditarian assumptions by demonstrating that standard IQ databases contain severe methodological flaws, cultural biases, and sampling errors. Additionally, rapid historical rises in IQ scores (the Flynn effect) point directly to environmental factors, such as access to education and healthcare, rather than evolutionary adaptations. Mainstream evolutionary biology indicates that human cognitive evolution was driven by social complexity and cultural adaptability, traits shared across all human populations.

Despite the lack of empirical backing, pseudo-scientific race narratives persist through dedicated funding networks, non-traditional publishing venues, and social media platforms. Researchers emphasize that academic freedom requires methodological rigor and peer evaluation, distinguishing validated scientific inquiry from unsupported ideological claims.

Who's involved: Authors Jonathan R. Goodman (University of Cambridge) and Rebecca Sear (Brunel University of London), drawing on research by Richard Lewontin and historical context involving Nathan Cofnas, the Human Diversity Foundation, and the journal Intelligence.

## ChatGPT shrunk down to run on a Super Nintendo

**Category:** Technical Release  |  **Found via:** The Register

This release details the creation of 'ChatGPT for SNES,' a project that adapts a large language model to run within the severe hardware constraints of a 1990s Super Nintendo Entertainment System. Traditional modern AI models rely on powerful cloud servers with gigabytes or terabytes of memory. To bridge this gap, creator Benj Edwards used model distillation—taking a massive 120-billion-parameter open-weight model (GPT-OSS-120b) and compressing its architecture into a tiny 2.2-million-parameter version with an 8,000-word vocabulary.

To overcome hardware limitations, the project uses two approaches. The lightweight 'pure' version runs in a 4MB ROM file, generating text directly from the miniature model's parameters. To compensate for the model's small memory footprint and frequent factual errors, a second 19MB version integrates a lookup database ('fact book') derived from 150,000 simple Wikipedia entry introductions. Running the software on original hardware requires custom flash cartridges (such as the FXPak Pro or SD2SNES) that support specialized coprocessors like the Super FX chip for processing acceleration and the MSU-1 chip for enhanced data handling. The project demonstrates how extreme model quantization and distillation can push interactive text generation onto low-power vintage embedded hardware.

Who's involved: Retro tech enthusiast Benj Edwards, utilizing open-weight model data derived from OpenAI.
