## Open models can do 90 percent of agentic work at a twentieth of the cost of a frontier LLM: Gartner

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** The Register

Artificial intelligence deployment in corporate enterprise environments is undergoing a significant strategic shift. As companies integrate AI agents into daily operations to handle multi-step workflows, the cost of running proprietary, massive frontier models—such as those developed by OpenAI or Anthropic—for every single task has become financially unsustainable. Gartner, a leading IT research and advisory firm, highlights this trend by forecasting that 90% of enterprise agentic workflow steps will run on open-weight models by 2030.

To understand why this shift is happening, it helps to break down how agentic workflows operate. An AI agent performing a complex business task does not execute one massive reasoning operation. Instead, it breaks the task into dozens or hundreds of minor, repetitive steps, such as classifying incoming data, passing information between systems, calling specific software tools, and validating entries. These routine steps do not require cutting-edge reasoning capabilities. Gartner's analysts point out that smaller, domain-specific open-weight models can perform these routine execution tasks just as effectively as premium frontier models, but at a fraction of the cost—ranging from a quarter to one-twentieth of the price.

This disparity in operational costs is dramatically altering the economic calculus for enterprise AI investments. Rather than relying on expensive APIs hosted by third-party model providers, businesses are increasingly running localized or open-weight models for routine operations. Doing so not only lowers operational expenses significantly but also addresses crucial data privacy concerns. When enterprises send routine data through commercial cloud APIs, they inadvertently risk sending sensitive operational history outside their corporate perimeter. Running open-weight models on-premises or within controlled environments allows organizations to retain full ownership and security over their workflow data.

Ultimately, this trend signals that individual AI models are rapidly becoming commodities. The real competitive advantage is shifting toward "harnesses"—the orchestration frameworks that manage agent fleets, enforce guardrails, and dynamically route tasks to the most cost-effective model required for the job. Frontier models will remain vital for high-level reasoning and research, but open-weight models will dominate daily enterprise execution.

Who's involved: Gartner (Mark Petty, Raymond Paquet).

## Architect creates open-source replacement for VM migration tool VMware has hidden

**Category:** Technical Release  |  **Found via:** The Register

Virtualization management experienced a major disruption when VMware restricted public downloads of its Virtual Disk Development Kit (VDDK). VDDK is a set of proprietary C-based software libraries and utilities that allow software programs to directly read, write, and manipulate virtual machine (VM) disk files stored in vSphere environments. Many IT organizations and third-party software tools relied heavily on these libraries to perform routine backups and to migrate running workloads off VMware onto rival hypervisors like KVM, Proxmox VE, or Apache CloudStack. By restricting VDDK downloads strictly to authorized partners, VMware made it significantly harder for organizations to migrate their data or run open-source migration pipelines.

To solve this roadblock, cloud architect Andrija Panić developed OpenVDDK, an open-source, independent replacement for VMware’s shared disk library (libvixDiskLib.so.8.0.3). Built completely from scratch using Go and wrapped in a thin C compatibility layer, OpenVDDK exposes the exact same C interfaces, symbol versions, and library name as VMware's native library. This architecture allows it to function as a seamless "drop-in" replacement without requiring modifications to existing migration tools. It was designed through clean-room reverse engineering and public documentation, ensuring it contains no proprietary VMware code.

Technically, OpenVDDK focuses specifically on read-only operations necessary for data extraction and migration rather than write access. When backup frameworks or migration engines (such as virt-v2v, nbdkit, or Red Hat Forklift) attempt to load the standard VMware disk library, OpenVDDK interceptively handles the disk-reading commands, communicating directly with vSphere, vCenter, or standalone ESXi hosts across various vSphere versions (from 6.7 U3 up to 9.1.1).

By restoring unrestricted access to virtual disk data, OpenVDDK removes vendor lock-in created by the download restrictions. It enables open-source virtualization platforms and cloud providers to smoothly import VMware workloads, preserving data portability and giving enterprises full control over where their virtual infrastructure runs.

Who's involved: Andrija Panić (cloud architect), VMware (Broadcom), Red Hat, Apache CloudStack, Proxmox VE.

## Google takes Dreambeans to India in bid to bring AI-generated personal stories to the masses

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** TechCrunch

Google is expanding its experimental artificial intelligence application, Dreambeans, to India as its first international market outside the United States. Dreambeans operates differently from standard conversational AI assistants like ChatGPT or Claude. Instead of waiting for a user to type in a prompt, the app proactively scans data from connected Google services—such as Google Calendar, Gmail, Google Photos, Search, and YouTube—to generate customized, illustrated, and animated personal visual stories about the user's daily life and interests. 

This expansion signals a strategic push by major technology companies to test consumer-facing, highly personalized AI applications in massive digital growth markets. India represents Google's second-largest internet user base, featuring a high concentration of users who actively use multiple Google services simultaneously. To serve this demographic, Google updated Dreambeans to support 13 local Indian languages, including Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, Bengali, and Punjabi, alongside English.

However, the rollout highlights several key challenges facing consumer AI adoption in international markets. First, user engagement with hyper-personalized storytelling apps remains uncertain; data cited in the report shows U.S. downloads dropped sharply after an initial surge. Second, cultural and linguistic nuances present technical hurdles. In India, multilinguality and language-mixing (such as blending Hindi and English into "Hinglish") are common, but Dreambeans currently forces users to select only one language for generated stories. 

Finally, the reliance on sensitive personal account data through Google's "Personal Intelligence" backend raises significant data privacy and emotional sensitivity concerns. Connecting email threads, calendars, and personal photos to an automated generation engine introduces risks of resurfacing distressing memories or misinterpreting private details. Google addresses this by allowing users to toggle specific service connections, delete data, and opt out of business-related topics, keeping the service restricted exclusively to individual consumer accounts.

Who's involved: Google, Google Labs, Gozde Oznur (Google Product Manager), AISIN, and Sensor Tower.

## Toyota shows off SoC that stores model weights and its OS on integrated memory

**Category:** Technical Release  |  **Found via:** The Register

Japanese automotive components manufacturer AISIN, in which Toyota is the major shareholder, alongside researchers at Tohoku University, has introduced a prototype System-on-Chip (SoC) designed specifically for edge computing in autonomous vehicles and robotics. Traditionally, artificial intelligence applications rely on processors that fetch machine learning model parameters (known as model weights) and operating system instructions from external Dynamic Random-Access Memory (DRAM). This constant data transfer between external memory sticks and the central processor creates a performance bottleneck, consumes considerable electrical power, increases physical space requirements, and exposes systems to high DRAM component costs.

To solve this memory bottleneck in real-time, computer-vision tasks, AISIN built a integrated silicon design using a 16-nanometer process that incorporates dual-core Arm processors alongside on-chip Spin-Transfer Torque Magnetic RAM (STT-MRAM). By embedding non-volatile STT-MRAM directly onto the semiconductor die, the chip stores both its Linux operating system kernel and the AI model weights directly within the processor unit itself, eliminating the need to communicate over slow external memory buses.

Because integrated MRAM capacity is limited compared to bulky external RAM modules, the engineering team designed a novel hybrid quantization engine. Standard AI models often calculate mathematical operations using 16-bit precision. AISIN’s chip combines 8-bit and 16-bit multiplication techniques dynamically across different layers of the neural network. This hybrid approach compresses the model weight footprint of an object-detection model like Tiny YOLOv3 by 53 percent—reducing it from 17.7 megabytes down to 9.36 megabytes—without degrading overall detection accuracy.

The resulting hardware architecture enables extremely fast startup and execution times, reaching kernel initialization in just 65.7 milliseconds after power-on, which is 38 times faster than system designs utilizing external memory. This architectural shift provides the low latency and low power consumption required for safety-critical physical AI systems, such as automotive automated emergency braking and real-time obstacle avoidance.

Who's involved: Toyota, AISIN, Tohoku University, Arm, Nvidia, and Qualcomm.

## Ukraine’s new pocket-sized gadget turns Russian drones’ cameras against them

**Category:** Technical Release  |  **Found via:** TechCrunch

Modern reconnaissance and strike drones often transmit real-time video back to their operators over unencrypted radio frequencies. Ukrainian hardware startup CODE380 has released the D·Vision SDR, a compact, 10-ounce software-defined radio receiver designed to intercept and decode these unencrypted analog camera signals in real time. Software-defined radio technology uses software running on embedded processors to process radio signals instead of relying on fixed hardware circuitry. This design allows the device to rapidly tune across a broad spectrum of radio frequencies, including non-standard transmission bands recently adopted by Russian drone units such as the Molniya system.

By deploying this device, battlefield personnel solve the critical problem of early tactical detection and situational awareness without lugging around heavy equipment. Frontline troops can intercept the drone operator's unencrypted live video feed, allowing them to determine the aircraft's trajectory, identify its intended target, and take defensive cover prior to an attack. However, while this mechanism provides an immediate defense against standard radio transmissions, its effectiveness is intrinsically temporary. Electronic warfare environments continually adapt; as drone operators transition to encrypted digital feeds or fully autonomous AI-guided systems that operate without radio transmissions, traditional signal interception tools will face declining utility.

Who's involved: CODE380, Serhii "Flash" Beskrestnov (radio specialist and advisor to Ukraine's president), and Defense News.

## "At Home in Space" opens, caps Air and Space Museum renovation

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** Ars Technica

The Smithsonian's National Air and Space Museum in Washington, D.C., has completed a massive, seven-year, nearly $1 billion renovation of its flagship facility. The final piece of this overhaul is a new permanent exhibition titled "At Home in Space," which focuses on human spaceflight achievements over the past 50 years, emphasizing how humanity transitioned from brief orbital visits to establishing a sustained presence in low-Earth orbit.

Historically, spaceflight exhibits centered on early race-to-space milestones, such as Project Mercury or the Apollo Moon landings. "At Home in Space" shifts the museum's narrative framework toward space habitability and long-duration spaceflight. The exhibit chronicles the infrastructure and technology required to maintain human life off Earth, serving as a conceptual bridge to future crewed missions targeting the Moon and Mars. It showcases critical real-world artifacts, including a fully flown Extravehicular Mobility Unit (EMU) spacesuit, flight garments worn by pioneering astronauts Guion Bluford and Sally Ride, and recovered structural debris from the Space Shuttles Challenger and Columbia.

Beyond hardware displays, the gallery relies on immersive and technical artifacts to convey life in microgravity. Visitors can walk through a full-scale replica of the International Space Station's (ISS) U.S. Destiny laboratory module, featuring real tools used for space biology like the miniPCR gene amplifier and MinION DNA sequencer. It also highlights modern manufacturing advances, displaying the first 3D printer flown in space. A placeholder model currently occupies space reserved for the real Artemis I Orion capsule, which completed an uncrewed circumlunar flight in 2022 and will eventually anchor the exhibit.

For the museum and aerospace industry, finishing this extensive renovation signifies a modern reset for public space education. The updated galleries accommodate growing public interest in contemporary commercial and international space exploration. By showing how astronauts eat, work, and conduct science off-planet, the exhibit frames long-duration space habitation not as science fiction, but as an ongoing engineering project.

Who's involved: Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum, NASA, Jennifer Levasseur (curator), Chris Browne (museum director), Redwire (formerly Made In Space), and Lockheed Martin.

## Apple is ‘not able to predict’ when it’ll have enough iPhone Duos to go around

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** The Verge

Apple CEO John Ternus announced that the company is currently unable to predict when supply will meet the strong demand for its newly released foldable smartphone, the iPhone Duo. Launched at a price point of $1,999, the device represents Apple's entry into the foldable hardware market, featuring a 5.4-inch external display and a 7.6-inch main inner screen. However, high customer demand paired with persistent global supply chain bottlenecks has caused preorders to rapidly exhaust inventory, pushing estimated delivery dates into 2027 for some customers.

A primary driver behind the supply constraints and hardware cost pressures is an industry-wide price hike in memory components. Dynamic Random-Access Memory (DRAM) and NAND flash storage costs have experienced significant increases due to supply-side shortages and changing semiconductor market dynamics. Ternus characterized these elevated memory prices as unprecedented, indicating that manufacturing input costs will likely escalate further into the final calendar quarter. To cushion these operational expenses, Apple implemented price adjustments across multiple device lines, including higher initial prices for flagship models like the iPhone 18 Pro tier.

Beyond component pricing, high-end consumer hardware production faces challenges when manufacturing complex form factors. Foldable smartphones require specialized assembly techniques, flexible OLED panels, and complex mechanical hinge mechanisms, which typically yield lower initial production rates compared to standard rigid glass slabs. Additionally, Apple confirmed it is addressing early hardware issues regarding reports of inner display cracking by offering replacements for affected units.

Despite these supply chain hurdles and elevated component costs, Apple recorded strong revenue performance, driven by sales of its premium hardware. However, the situation underscores how global semiconductor inventory cycles and advanced hardware manufacturing complexity can impact product availability and price structures, even for tech hardware makers with immense supply chain influence.

Who's involved: Apple and John Ternus (Apple CEO).

## Why large dogs age faster than small ones, and other October research highlights

**Category:** Research  |  **Found via:** Ars Technica

Scientific research frequently reveals surprising mechanisms behind biological and physical phenomena. A recent roundup of scientific discoveries highlights several notable findings across biology, physics, materials science, and forensic history. First, researchers studying canine longevity investigated why large dogs age faster and die younger than small dogs, bucking the typical mammalian pattern. By analyzing DNA methylation across hundreds of dogs from the Dog Aging Project, scientists discovered that giant dog breeds lose control over "jumping genes"—specifically a class known as LINE1s—much faster than smaller breeds. As DNA methylation degrades, these mobile genetic sequences move across chromosomes, causing cellular damage and age-related disease. This provides a clear molecular signature explaining the size-lifespan tradeoff in canines and opens new avenues for understanding mammalian aging mechanisms. Second, in fundamental physics, experimenters achieved a major breakthrough by capturing and isolating a single positron—an antimatter counterpart to an electron—for several months. Sourced from sodium-22 nuclei, the positron was trapped in a ultra-high vacuum using electric and magnetic fields and cooled to 300 millikelvins. Keeping the particle in its quantum ground state for extended periods allows scientists to make ultra-precise measurements of its magnetic moment using microwaves. Comparing these properties against those of electrons tests the fundamental symmetry predicted by the Standard Model of particle physics, potentially offering clues as to why matter dominates the universe over antimatter. Third, materials scientists developed custom invisible inks, named PhotoTat, made from color-changing dyes encased in tiny PMMA polymer spheres. These inks remain completely invisible under normal light but glow in vibrant primary colors under ultraviolet illumination before fading after a few hours. This advance offers both aesthetic freedom for body art without societal stigma and practical medical utility, such as creating temporary, invisible alignment marks for patients undergoing targeted radiation therapy. Who's involved: Arizona State University, Dog Aging Project, Northwestern University, University of Colorado Boulder, and Westminster Abbey archaeologists.

## Apple set an iPhone sales record even without a new standard iPhone

Apple reported its fiscal fourth-quarter financial results, demonstrating remarkable resilience and market dominance despite shifting product strategies and global supply chain headwinds. The consumer electronics giant generated $57.58 billion in iPhone revenue alone—a 17 percent year-over-year increase—driven primarily by strong demand for its higher-end flagship devices, the iPhone 18 Pro and Pro Max, alongside broader price adjustments across older hardware lines. What makes this financial performance notable within the mobile device landscape is that Apple achieved record quarterly iPhone revenue without introducing a new standard base-model smartphone during its autumn product refresh cycle. Instead, consumer demand gravitated toward premium tier offerings. Additionally, Apple debuted its first foldable device, the iPhone Duo, though constrained supply and extended shipping estimates meant its financial contribution was largely excluded from this quarter's figures. Overall corporate revenue rose 12 percent to $114.5 billion, buoyed by a 24 percent jump in Mac sales and continued strength in its high-margin Services segment, which brought in $31.57 billion through App Store fees and digital subscriptions. This earnings report highlights key structural trends in the tech hardware industry. Hardware manufacturers are grappling with increased component expenses, particularly rising memory costs, as well as lingering supply constraints. Apple successfully managed these operational challenges through careful inventory management and lower non-memory component costs, while successfully shifting consumer mix toward higher-priced devices. The strategy demonstrates that consumer tech companies can expand profit margins and revenue even amid supply challenges by capitalizing on brand equity and premium feature differentiation. Moving forward, Apple's ability to maintain high revenue growth across both hardware and recurring software services provides a clear signal of strong consumer engagement despite macroeconomic pressures.
