## Smartphone shipments face another bleak year before modest 2028 comeback

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** The Register

The global smartphone industry is navigating an extended downturn, driven by severe semiconductor supply chain pressures. Global handset shipments are projected to drop sharply through 2026 and 2027 before experiencing a modest recovery in 2028. Understanding this decline requires looking at the broader tech landscape: memory manufacturers are reallocating silicon production capacity toward high-margin enterprise AI infrastructure, such as high-bandwidth memory and server-grade storage, leaving less capacity for consumer-grade components like low-power double data rate (LPDDR) DRAM and NAND flash. This structural pivot has triggered a massive price surge in memory components, which in some cases now account for up to 60 percent of the manufacturing bill of materials for budget mobile devices. Consequently, phone makers are forced to raise retail prices, downgrade device memory specifications, or eliminate entry-level models altogether.

This squeeze has significant macroeconomic implications across the consumer electronics landscape. Low-cost and mid-range devices in price-sensitive markets bear the heaviest burden, leading to deferred consumer upgrades and permanently lost demand at the bottom end of the market. Hardware vendors face squeezed profit margins and shrinking shipment volumes, while consumers are keeping existing devices longer. Even as supply bottlenecks ease and memory prices normalize toward 2028, market intelligence indicates that overall volume will remain well below previous historical peak levels. In the longer term, handset manufacturers hope next-generation networking standards like 6G will rekindle widespread replacement cycles, but structural competition for chip production between enterprise artificial intelligence datacenters and personal mobility devices represents a long-term shift in global hardware manufacturing dynamics.

Who's involved: Counterpoint Research, IDC, Omdia, and major smartphone original equipment manufacturers (OEMs).

## Bill for Birmingham's Oracle debacle reaches £128M

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** The Register

Birmingham City Council's ongoing project to replace its legacy enterprise resource planning (ERP) system has reached a critical cost milestone of £128.1 million. ERP platforms form the digital backbone of large organizations, integrating complex operational functions such as payroll, human resources, general accounting, and vendor procurement into a single unified database. When migrating from a legacy platform (in this case, an older SAP installation) to a modern cloud-based system like Oracle Fusion, organizations face immense technical and operational challenges. A core mistake in large-scale IT migrations is attempting to customize enterprise cloud software to match legacy custom workflows rather than adapting internal organizational processes to fit standard, out-of-the-box software functions.

In Birmingham's case, custom engineering—such as a custom-built bank reconciliation feature—failed to operate correctly after the initial 2022 deployment. This left council administrators unable to accurately track cash reserves, balance ledger accounts, or generate auditable financial statements, contributing directly to the municipality declaring effective bankruptcy. The council was ultimately forced to abandon the custom implementation and undertake a complete reimplementation using standard software configurations alongside supplemental financial tools like Civica Income Management. The broader industry implications serve as a cautionary case study for public sector digital transformations globally. Beyond the direct implementation costs—which expanded to over six times the original £19.96 million budget estimate—independent audits suggest the total financial damage, including uncollected local taxes, manual workaround labor, and unrealized efficiency savings, will exceed £200 million. It highlights the severe financial and operational risks associated with poor governance, ignoring technical readiness warnings, and over-customizing standardized enterprise software platforms.

Who's involved: Birmingham City Council, Oracle, SAP, Civica, and the Audit Reform Lab at the University of Sheffield.

**Source:** https://www.theregister.com/public-sector/2026/11/27/bill-for-birminghams-oracle-debacle-reaches-128m/5312563

## UK government rejects national consultation on AI datacenters

**Category:** Policy / Security  |  **Found via:** The Register

The UK government has formally rejected a public petition, backed by over 39,000 signatures, demanding a national public consultation on the environmental impacts of expanding AI datacenters. Protesters and environmental groups like Protect the Wild argue that large compute infrastructure threatens local biodiversity, drains local power grids, and accelerates greenhouse gas emissions. Instead of conducting a broad nationwide review, ministers determined that environmental impacts must continue to be evaluated on a project-by-project basis through existing local planning procedures, alongside a draft datacenter planning policy consultation.

To balance rapid infrastructure expansion with public concern, the government pointed to its Data Centre Covenant. This voluntary framework outlines guidelines requiring operators to cover local infrastructure costs, invest in communities, create jobs, and maintain environmental standards. However, the covenant lacks concrete enforcement mechanisms or specific statutory biodiversity safeguards, and mandatory compliance applies only to facilities deemed strategic to qualify for government assistance. Critics contend that relying exclusively on local planning decisions prevents regulators from measuring cumulative, nationwide impacts on national net-zero goals.

This decision affects tech companies planning UK datacenter developments, local communities near proposed sites, and environmental advocacy groups. Looking ahead, local planning authorities will remain the primary battleground for datacenter approvals, while the government faces continued scrutiny over its pro-growth AI strategy, which prioritizes rapid tech expansion over national environmental reviews.

Who's involved: UK Department for Business, Innovation, Science and Trade (BIST), Protect the Wild, Natural England, and UK datacenter operators.

## Intel faced a choice: Give Trump 10% or face the consequences

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** Ars Technica

Unredacted court documents from a shareholder lawsuit reveal intense political pressure exerted by the Trump administration on major US semiconductor firms. In August 2025, Commerce Department officials demanded that Intel yield a 10 percent equity stake—valued at approximately $11 billion—to the federal government without direct cash payment. The administration used the release of previously approved CHIPS Act grants as leverage while raising security concerns regarding CEO Lip-Bu Tan's past venture investments in China. Despite internal reluctance from Intel's board regarding the terms, the company ultimately agreed to the deal to secure critical funding and political alignment.

This reporting highlights an unprecedented shift in US industrial policy, moving away from standard market-based incentives or national security reviews toward direct executive intervention in corporate ownership and executive governance. The administration pursued similar arrangements with Nvidia, demanding revenue-sharing cuts on specialized AI chip sales to China and pressuring its board over executive leadership. A shareholder suit led by investor Richard Paisner alleges these coercions constitute corporate extortion and regulatory overreach.

For the technology sector, this trend signals an evolving regulatory environment where domestic capital access, executive tenure, and export permissions are increasingly tied to direct transactional agreements with the federal government. Technology firms operating in sensitive sectors like AI and hardware manufacturing must now navigate complex political risks alongside standard economic factors.

Who's involved: Intel Corporation, Nvidia, Donald Trump, Lip-Bu Tan, Jensen Huang, David Shapiro, Howard Lutnick, and Richard Paisner.
