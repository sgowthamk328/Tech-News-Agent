## When datacenters lose power, generators should kick in. That didn’t happen for Google in London

**Category:** Technical Release  |  **Found via:** The Register

Modern data centers rely on layered power systems to remain online during municipal grid failures. Usually, a facility uses duplicate power distribution pathways alongside Uninterruptible Power Supply (UPS) battery systems that bridge immediate gaps, followed by diesel backup generators managed by automated industrial microcomputers called Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs). The PLC continuously monitors grid voltage; if power drops, it commands the backup generators to ignite and take over the load before the UPS battery capacity expires.

During an outage at Google Cloud’s europe-west-2-b London facility, this failover mechanism failed due to a combination of active maintenance work and a design logic flaw. One primary utility line (Feeder B) was opened for maintenance following a load test. When an underground cable on the remaining active line (Feeder A) physically severed, the total electrical grid supply went dead, and the temporary UPS system engaged as designed to provide five minutes of emergency power. However, due to a voltage-sensing design flaw and improper configuration within the dual-redundant PLC, the system incorrectly calculated that a healthy utility source was still connected. Because the logic loop falsely identified active utility power, the PLC never transmitted the automatic start signal to the standby generator plant, causing the UPS batteries to deplete entirely and taking major services offline.

To solve this issue, Google reprogrammed the PLC logic to ensure accurate power-state detection under all maintenance configurations, re-architected its local electrical distribution layout, and established expanded platform-wide infrastructure stress tests. Additionally, Google is introducing an automated alert mechanism to immediately notify enterprise clients during critical availability zone incidents.

Who's involved: Google Cloud, affected enterprise customers (including Lloyds, HSBC, Royal Mail, and Revolut).

## VMware quietly offers European users extended sales and support for low-end vSphere

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** The Register

Broadcom’s VMware unit has quietly updated its product roadmap to extend the sales and support lifecycle for lower-tier virtualization software specifically for customers in the European Economic Area (EEA). Under this revised timeline, vSphere Standard 8 and vSphere Enterprise Plus 8 will remain available for purchase in Europe until October 31, 2027, with extended support bundled into active subscriptions until October 31, 2028. This extension pushes the end-of-life date for these entry-level hypervisor packages more than a full year beyond the general support deadline for VMware’s flagship Enterprise offerings, such as Cloud Foundation 5.x and vSphere 8. 

This decision carries significant weight for enterprise IT departments across Europe. Following Broadcom’s acquisition of VMware, the company aggressively overhauled its licensing model, phasing out standalone perpetual licenses in favor of expensive, bundled subscription suites like VMware Cloud Foundation (VCF). This shift created widespread friction, particularly among small-to-medium enterprises and cloud service providers who only required basic server virtualization rather than full-stack cloud management platforms. Analysts strongly attribute this European extension to regulatory pressure, notably an ongoing antitrust investigation opened by the European Commission after complaints from the Cloud Infrastructure Services Providers in Europe (CISPE) lobby group, as well as recent court rulings in EU member states enforcing existing support contracts.

For the broader industry, this policy change demonstrates how stringent European regulatory frameworks can force multinational technology conglomerates to grant regional concessions. By granting up to an extra 12 months, European organizations gain crucial breathing room to either plan migration paths to VCF 9 or transition workloads to alternative hypervisors such as Proxmox or Microsoft Hyper-V. However, enterprise software analysts caution that organizations outside the EEA remain excluded from these extensions unless local regulators follow Europe's precedent. It also underscores a growing operational divide between European infrastructure standards and the rest of the global enterprise market.

Who's involved: Broadcom, VMware, European Commission, Cloud Infrastructure Services Providers in Europe (CISPE), and Michael Warrilow.

## Microsoft admits certifications can be a drag, launches creds that recognize skills

**Category:** Technical Release  |  **Found via:** The Register

Microsoft has introduced a new credential system called 'Pro Badges,' designed to evaluate and certify technical skills based on real-time software usage rather than traditional, high-stakes examination testing. The initiative debuts with a credential for GitHub Copilot, Microsoft's AI-powered coding assistant, with plans to expand the badge model across other enterprise products in the future.

Traditional technical certifications typically require developers and system administrators to take scheduled, proctored exams that test theoretical knowledge and memorization. Industry professionals have long criticized this approach because exam scores do not always reflect real-world problem-solving abilities or daily workflow efficiency. Microsoft’s Pro Badge system addresses this problem by replacing exam-day testing with continuous telemetry and activity analysis. 

The underlying mechanism relies on an automated 'proficiency engine' integrated into GitHub. When a user logs in and links their profile at learn.github.com, the engine systematically tracks interaction signals from their daily work. It evaluates hands-on usage across multiple repositories over extended periods, assessing key activities such as web-based collaboration, Integrated Development Environment (IDE) feature utilization, context management, agent configuration, and model selection. The system categorizes user skill into five tiers: beginner, intermediate, proficient, advanced, and expert. Once a developer demonstrates sustained practical execution at the 'proficient' level, the system automatically awards the Pro Badge, which can then be displayed directly on the user's LinkedIn profile without paying fees or completing study guides.

By monitoring actual productivity rather than test preparation, Microsoft aims to offer employers a dynamic, verified metric of practical capability. This skill-verification architecture allows technical professionals to build recognized credentials seamlessly within their existing workflows, reducing certification fatigue while helping organizations measure actual tool adoption across development teams.

Who's involved: Microsoft, GitHub, Cameron Percy (General Manager for Content and Insights at Microsoft), and LinkedIn.

## Apple is reportedly cutting budgets for its internal AI usage

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** TechCrunch

To understand the broader implications of Apple's reported move, it is helpful to look at how modern artificial intelligence tools are purchased and consumed by enterprises. When developers or corporate employees use generative AI models to write code, design hardware, or draft documents, the underlying service typically bills based on computational volume—frequently measured in 'tokens' (chunks of text or data processed). Earlier phases of the generative AI boom saw companies aggressively encourage widespread adoption under an ethos dubbed 'tokenmaxxing,' where teams were told to use AI tools as much as possible to unlock speculative productivity gains.

However, running advanced AI models is computationally intensive and rapidly becomes expensive when scaled across thousands of employees. Apple is reportedly responding to these compounding costs by curbing internal spending. In October, the company lowered its daily AI usage allowance for engineering teams from $300 to $200 per day, with further cuts down to $100 per day planned, and limits for non-engineering staff potentially dropping to $25 per day. 

This shift highlights an emerging period of fiscal reckoning for the broader tech industry. Enterprise leaders are confronting a persistent mismatch between the steep operational expense of third-party AI compute and the demonstrable, measurable gains in worker productivity. By introducing hard daily caps on API usage, Apple is transitioning from an exploratory, spend-heavy posture to an era of strict rationing. For the wider AI ecosystem, this signals that enterprise buyers are demanding immediate return on investment rather than subsidizing open-ended experimentation, putting downward pressure on AI infrastructure providers to deliver more cost-efficient, quantifiable value.

Who's involved: Apple and Bloomberg (which first reported the spending cap reductions).

*The exact dollar figures for internal daily spending caps ($300 down to $200, $100, and $25) are reported by Bloomberg via sources, while Apple itself has not officially confirmed the cuts.*

## After the midterms, a bigger clash over data centers could be coming

**Category:** Policy / Security  |  **Found via:** TechCrunch

Modern artificial intelligence depends heavily on vast physical infrastructure known as data centers—warehouses filled with tens of thousands of specialized computing servers. These facilities consume enormous volumes of electricity and water for cooling, and in many regions, they rely on localized fossil fuel power sources like gas turbines. Following recent U.S. midterm elections, Democrats captured control of Congress, while a surge of grassroots political opposition emerged across states like Ohio, Texas, and Virginia, turning data center development into a major ballot-box battleground.

What changed is that voter backlash against the unchecked expansion of computing infrastructure has crossed traditional party lines. Local residents are protesting utility bill hikes resulting from power grid upgrades, environmental pollution, and non-transparent zoning agreements handled under non-disclosure pacts. Exit polls revealed that nearly 80% of surveyed voters oppose local data center construction, and several candidates who ran on anti-buildout platforms secured office, including local bans passed in Ohio counties.

Those affected include hyperscale tech developers like OpenAI, Nvidia, Meta, Amazon, and xAI, which now face substantial political headwinds. With a divided federal government and energized local resistance, tech companies can expect construction delays, tighter environmental oversight, and potential legislative roadblocks. Next steps include pushback on federal energy rollbacks, potential local and state construction moratoriums, and federal legislative proposals like the GRID Savings Act, which seeks to prevent tech giants from shifting electrical grid expansion costs onto local residential utility payers. If passed, these guardrails could force companies to fund their own clean energy infrastructure and submit to binding local community approval.

Who's involved: U.S. Congressional lawmakers (including Sen. Bernie Sanders, Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, and Sen. Martin Heinrich), Color of Change, OpenAI, Nvidia, Meta, and xAI.

## X says its updated web app is way faster

**Category:** Technical Release  |  **Found via:** The Verge

Modern web platforms often grow complex by relying on separate server architectures to handle different tasks—such as rendering pages for logged-in users, serving search engine crawlers, and generating preview cards for shared links. Over time, maintaining these distinct infrastructure pathways introduces performance bottlenecks, leading to long initial loading times for users on slow or high-latency internet connections.

X (formerly Twitter) addressed this inefficiency by completely rewriting its web application infrastructure. The primary engineering overhaul involved consolidating its three backend server setups—previously divided among authenticated users, search crawlers, and link prefetching—into a single unified server architecture. By streamlining server responses and optimizing client-side code execution, the platform drastically reduced initial page render delays. On slow network connections, initial profile render times dropped from over 22 seconds on the legacy web framework down to 2.7 seconds on the updated codebase. 

This redesign solves the issue of heavy network overhead and bloated client loads. Instead of fetching disparate data assets across multiple round trips, the unified architecture delivers essential page content immediately, establishing feature parity across logged-in and logged-out views while significantly lowering load latency globally. The updated web app is initially deployed to logged-out visitors and search crawlers while being rolled out incrementally to logged-in accounts worldwide.

Who's involved: X (formerly Twitter), Yagiz Nizipli (Principal Engineer at X), and Jane Manchun Wong (Security Researcher).

**Source:** https://www.theverge.com/tech/1027113/x-says-its-updated-web-app-is-way-faster

*The claimed reduction in profile load time from 22 seconds to 2.7 seconds is reported directly from X engineer benchmarks and is unverified by independent third-party performance testing.*

## Nintendo's biggest ROM chip supplier is bowing out of the business

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** Ars Technica

The global surge in artificial intelligence infrastructure has driven unprecedented demand for advanced memory technologies, such as high-bandwidth memory, NAND flash, and solid-state drives. Semiconductor manufacturers are reallocating their production lines toward these high-margin memory formats to satisfy AI data center clients. However, this capacity reallocation creates a severe squeeze for legacy component supply chains that rely on silicon wafer space, including Read-Only Memory (ROM) chips used in physical gaming hardware.

Taiwanese chip manufacturer Macronix, long serving as the main ROM supplier for Nintendo Switch game cartridges, announced it is ending ROM production to focus wafer capacity on more profitable NOR, NAND, and eMMC products. This strategic exit highlights a broader industry trend where legacy physical media formats are squeezed out by high-tech compute demand. Because manufacturing capacity remains constrained, producing low-margin ROM chips becomes economically unviable for semiconductor foundries.

For the video game industry, this supply shift directly impacts physical software distribution. While Nintendo has confirmed it intends to continue offering physical cartridges for its platforms, relying on secondary suppliers like Rohm and Lapis Semiconductor could raise unit manufacturing costs. Consequently, video game publishers may face higher production fees for physical releases, accelerating the broader market transition toward digital distribution models.

Who's involved: Nintendo, Macronix, Rohm, Lapis Semiconductor, and analyst David Gibson.

**Source:** https://arstechnica.com/gaming/2026/11/the-tech-memory-shortage-comes-for-nintendos-cartridge-supply-chain/

## Qualcomm is betting big on data centers as smartphone sales sink

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** The Verge

Semiconductor designer Qualcomm reported its fourth-quarter financial results for fiscal year 2026, revealing a strategic pivot to diversify its business away from mobile devices and toward artificial intelligence data centers. Traditionally, Qualcomm has relied heavily on selling processors and modems for smartphones. However, with global smartphone sales declining roughly 20 percent compared to 2025, the company is reallocating resources to meet the massive demand for hardware that powers cloud computing and AI workloads. Data centers are centralized computing facilities filled with servers that require specialized silicon to perform high-speed computations for training and running complex AI models. Qualcomm projects that revenue from its data center division will grow from $650 million in early 2027 to $15 billion by 2029, eventually accounting for roughly 30 percent of its total business. To achieve this, the chipmaker has entered multi-generation hardware partnerships with hyper-scale cloud infrastructure providers such as Amazon Web Services and Meta. This shift reflects a broader enterprise trend across the semiconductor industry, where legacy hardware makers are aggressively reshaping their product lines to capture spending generated by the artificial intelligence boom, competing directly with established server chip providers like AMD and Nvidia. Who's involved: Qualcomm, Amazon Web Services (AWS), Meta, and AMD.
