## Data centers are now a midterm issue, even in Brooklyn

**Category:** Policy / Security  |  **Found via:** TechCrunch

Across the United States, the physical infrastructure powering artificial intelligence has rapidly evolved from an obscure enterprise necessity into a prominent political battleground. In the lead-up to the midterm elections, local communities, advocacy groups, and voters have mobilized around the sudden influx of hyper-scale data centers. Rather than viewing these facilities purely as economic engines, residents and local representatives are raising alarms about strained electrical grids, environmental risks, public health hazards, and zoning maneuvers that allow multi-billion-dollar facilities to be constructed with minimal public oversight. According to findings cited from Data Center Watch, nearly $200 billion in data center initiatives were delayed, blocked, or halted in the first half of 2026 due to grassroots and political friction.

The policy shift centers on local control, transparency, and accountability. Historically, tech corporations secured agreements with regional authorities under non-disclosure agreements, leaving communities unaware of massive water and energy requirements until permits were finalized. In response, several states—including Virginia, Georgia, Pennsylvania, Texas, and New York—are introducing stricter regulatory guardrails, requiring clearer zoning definitions and public disclosures. The controversy spans diverse voting bases: in rural and industrial corridors, skepticism surrounds whether these facilities generate lasting employment after brief construction phases, while urban areas debate the broader social and financial impact of tech conglomerates.

Moving forward, technology providers face unprecedented operational headwinds. As data center expansion becomes an election-defining issue, incoming lawmakers will likely impose stricter utility oversight, mandatory community impact assessments, and limitations on municipal tax subsidies. Consequently, infrastructure planners will be forced to reconsider energy sourcing and engage in transparent public bargaining, ending the era of frictionless physical expansion for artificial intelligence.

Who's involved: TechCrunch reporter Dominic-Madori Davis, NYC Council member Chi Ossé, Newsreel founder Jack Brewster, and environmental and policy advocates Hilary Flint and Maia Woluchem.

## Binance CEO says 90 percent of stolen assets have been ‘replenished’

**Category:** Policy / Security  |  **Found via:** The Verge

Cryptocurrency exchange platform Binance has reported that it has replenished 90 percent of user assets taken during a record-breaking $6.3 billion cyber heist. Cryptocurrency exchanges operate as centralized custodial hubs where digital tokens are pooled and managed on behalf of retail and institutional users. When a security compromise occurs at this scale, it threatens not only the direct assets of the victims but also the solvency and fundamental trust required to keep a centralized exchange operational without triggering a systemic run on customer deposits.

In response to the exploit, Binance suspended customer withdrawals to isolate the affected infrastructure and prevent further unauthorized drainage of digital assets. The company's leadership confirmed that Binance is covering the losses directly from its own reserves to ensure account holders are made whole. According to executive statements, the remaining 10 percent of missing funds comprises less liquid cryptocurrency tokens, which require extended time to repurchase on secondary markets without inducing massive price slippage. Centralized platforms typically hold backstop funds, such as emergency reserves, specifically intended to absorb extreme losses resulting from security failures.

The immediate priority following this breach is the secure reopening of user withdrawal services. If an exchange fails to execute withdrawals predictably after a major hack, panic can lead to broad capital flight across the crypto asset sector. Regulators worldwide are closely watching security incidents of this magnitude, and the event will almost certainly accelerate global regulatory oversight, mandatory cold-storage reserve audits, and stricter statutory insurance mandates for centralized digital asset custodians. The incident highlights the enduring cybersecurity risks inherent to centralized pools of digital wealth.

## Google's AI satellite sends a very expensive haiku to Earth

**Category:** Technical Release  |  **Found via:** The Register

Google has achieved an operational milestone with Project Suncatcher, an experimental prototype designed to test the feasibility of orbital datacenters. Operating in low Earth orbit, the satellite successfully powered up its payload—comprising host servers, AMD processors, and Google's custom Trillium Cloud Tensor Processing Units (TPUs)—and conducted an artificial intelligence inference pass. Ground controllers routed a prompt requesting a poem to the orbiting craft as it passed over Svalbard, Norway, after which the onboard Gemma 4 open-weights model generated a three-line haiku and transmitted the resulting data tokens back to Earth via radio downlink.

Running large language model workloads directly in space serves to evaluate how commodity enterprise hardware copes with extreme operating conditions outside Earth's atmosphere. Terrestrial datacenters face mounting energy constraints, cooling bottlenecks, and land-use limits. Placing compute clusters into orbit presents access to continuous solar energy, but introduces harsh thermal fluctuations and cosmic radiation. Radiation poses a severe risk of single-event effects and silent data corruption, where stray subatomic particles alter bits inside computer memory without triggering standard hardware crash warnings. Preliminary proton beam evaluations indicated that the satellite's high-bandwidth memory begins exhibiting irregularities around two kilorads of radiation, but the estimated failure rate in orbit remains roughly one error per three million inferences, a tolerance level sufficient for non-critical query serving.

While this successful downlink demonstrates that modern deep-learning accelerators can operate functionally in low Earth orbit, significant economic hurdles remain before orbital computing rivals terrestrial server farms. The current cost per kilogram of launching orbital hardware makes space-based token processing orders of magnitude more expensive than ground-based cloud instances. Google's roadmap relies on future commercial rocket advances to sharply reduce orbital payload deployment pricing over the next decade. Next steps include testing inter-satellite networking between multiple spacecraft to form distributed, fault-tolerant orbital constellations.

Who's involved: Google, its Paradigms of Intelligence research team (led by Travis Beals), SpaceX, and AMD.

## Goth aesthetic gone wild: a user's self-inflicted black screen of non-death in Windows 3.1’s

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** The Register

Microsoft veteran software engineer Raymond Chen provided insight into the inner diagnostics of modern personal computing systems by recounting how an extreme customization mishap from the early computing era parallels contemporary operating system engineering challenges. Decades ago, an end-user running Windows 3.1 modified their system's graphical interface colors entirely to black—including backgrounds, buttons, title bars, and text. Upon subsequent reboots, the operating system displayed what appeared to be a catastrophic blank screen, concealing functional interface controls because every rendered graphic shared the identical black palette. The resolution required altering system configuration text files directly rather than navigating the invisible graphical user interface.

This historical mishap serves as a practical illustration of modern diagnostic challenges handled by the Windows kernel. When an operating system freezes or fails to draw visual output, human users typically react by depressing the physical power button on the computer chassis. Modern versions of Windows track this behavior: holding down the physical power button for eight seconds triggers an emergency kernel routine before cutting power entirely. The operating system designates this sequence as a long power button hold failure and attempts to capture a diagnostic kernel memory dump to help engineers determine why the system appeared unresponsive to the user.

Critically, these automated diagnostic dumps examine graphical framebuffer states to record whether every pixel on the monitor was completely black at the moment of the forced shutdown. Distinguishing between hardware freeze-ups, malfunctioning third-party video display drivers, and software rendering bugs allows operating system vendors to target automated telemetry investigations. For developers and hardware vendors, these mechanisms ensure that forced hardware shutdowns provide useful debugging telemetry rather than occurring as completely unrecoverable, untraceable black-box crashes across the diverse PC hardware ecosystem.

Who's involved: Microsoft and Microsoft senior software engineer Raymond Chen.

## Seabirds racing home over the ocean can get jet-lagged

**Category:** Research  |  **Found via:** Ars Technica

Researchers at the University of Oxford have discovered that wild seabirds called Manx shearwaters experience jet lag that temporarily disrupts their internal navigation system. Many animals, including birds, rely on a built-in biological clock to use the Sun as a compass. Because the Sun moves across the sky throughout the day, a bird's internal clock tells it what angle to fly relative to the Sun to stay on course. However, when these birds travel rapidly across longitudinal lines over long oceanic foraging trips—covering hundreds of kilometers in a single day—their circadian rhythm falls out of sync with the local time of their new position. For every degree of longitude traveled between sunrise and sunset, their internal clock drifts by four minutes.

This study is novel because it provides the first clear empirical evidence of jet lag affecting wild, free-ranging animals in their natural environment without human intervention. By analyzing two decades of GPS tracking data comprising over 3,600 trips, researchers observed that shearwaters consistently veered off course by up to 15 degrees in direct proportion to their predicted internal clock error. While the birds frequently check their position using an independent map-based sense and correct their path—adding only a few minimal kilometers to their journey—the fundamental bias persists.

This finding opens up new avenues for comparative biology and technology. Scientists can now explore whether animals living in high-latitude environments or long-distance migrators have evolved physiological mechanisms to mitigate circadian disruption. Additionally, understanding how biological systems combine multiple imperfect navigation signals—such as visual landmarks and shifted celestial compasses—can inform the design of bio-inspired autonomous navigation systems for robotics and drones.

Who's involved: Patrick Lewin, Tim Guilford, Chris Perrins, and researchers from the University of Oxford.

## The FDA wants to pull its approval of a drug. It’s not so easy.

**Category:** Policy / Security  |  **Found via:** Ars Technica

The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has formally proposed withdrawing approval for Tavneos (avacopan), an expensive medication for a rare autoimmune disease called ANCA-associated vasculitis. The regulatory shift occurred after unsealed legal documents revealed that the drug's original developer, ChemoCentryx, allegedly engaged in unblinded data manipulation and selected readjudication of trial outcomes after its pivotal study failed to show superior efficacy over standard care. Following reports of severe liver injury and safety concerns, the FDA concluded the drug lacks substantial evidence of efficacy and poses public health risks. European and UK regulators have already taken the medication off their markets, and the paper detailing the original clinical trial was recently retracted by the New England Journal of Medicine.

This regulatory battle primarily affects patients suffering from ANCA-associated vasculitis, who face the loss of a key alternative to high-dose steroid treatments, as well as pharmaceutical giant Amgen, which acquired ChemoCentryx for $4 billion in 2022 and relies on Tavneos for nearly half a billion dollars in annual revenue. Under statutory procedures, Amgen has submitted a 104-page response disputing the FDA's claims, arguing that underlying data was not altered and requesting a public hearing to defend the product's marketing authorization.

Next, the FDA must evaluate Amgen's submission to determine if a formal evidentiary hearing is warranted. If the hearing proceeds and the FDA ultimately issues a final withdrawal order, Amgen may file a lawsuit against the agency in federal court to block the decision. The case sets a crucial regulatory precedent for how stringently federal agencies will police post-approval clinical trial integrity and address data manipulation by small biotech developers.

Who's involved: US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), Amgen, ChemoCentryx, Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER), and Tracy Beth Høeg.

**Source:** https://arstechnica.com/science/2026/10/the-fda-wants-to-pull-its-approval-of-a-drug-its-not-so-easy/
