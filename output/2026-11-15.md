## SpaceX returned its Starship rocket on Saturday—but there's a catch

**Category:** Technical Release  |  **Found via:** Ars Technica

SpaceX accomplished a major milestone in aerospace engineering by successfully capturing its massive Starship upper stage using mechanical catch arms—often called "chopsticks"—attached to its launch tower at Starbase, Texas. Historically, spaceflight hardware has been single-use or required extensive, costly refurbishment. While NASA's Space Shuttle pioneered partial reusability, it landed as an unpowered glider and relied on fragile thermal protection systems that took months to repair. In contrast, SpaceX's architecture relies on powered retro-propulsion, using Raptor rocket engines to flip the rocket vertically at the last second and lower it directly into a landing structure.

Catching a rocket directly at the launch pad solves two critical bottlenecks in spaceflight economics: structural weight and turnaround time. Landing legs add significant mass to a vehicle, reducing the payload capacity it can carry into orbit. By eliminating landing legs entirely and delegating the landing impact forces to a ground structure, SpaceX preserves vehicle performance. Furthermore, landing directly back on the launch mount avoids sea transport recovery or long transit times, enabling rapid inspection, refueling, and relaunch.

This success serves as a key proof of concept for SpaceX's long-term goal of achieving full and rapid reuse. While thermal protection tiles remain a technical challenge to perfect over repeated re-entries, demonstrating precise guidance control to dock with sub-meter tolerances validates the fundamental mechanics of the architecture. If scaled reliably, this mechanism lowers the cost per pound of sending mass into orbit from tens of thousands of dollars to fractions of that amount, transforming orbit access from rare, expensive expeditions into high-frequency transport operations.

Who's involved: SpaceX, Elon Musk, and Ars Technica (reporting by Eric Berger).

## Watch SpaceX successfully execute the first ‘chopstick’ catch of Starship

**Category:** Technical Release  |  **Found via:** The Verge

When engineers design rockets intended for deep space exploration and heavy orbital payloads, every single kilogram of weight matters. Traditional reusable rocket designs rely on landing legs built directly onto the spacecraft. While effective, these onboard legs add significant weight, which reduces the amount of useful cargo, fuel, or satellites the vehicle can carry into space. To solve this fundamental constraint of rocket physics, SpaceX designed a revolutionary ground-based launch and landing architecture known as "Mechazilla"—a massive tower equipped with two large mechanical arms commonly referred to as "chopsticks."

Rather than carrying heavy landing gear into orbit and back through atmospheric reentry, the Starship vehicle relies on this external tower mechanism to catch it right out of the air as it returns to Earth. During a landing attempt, Starship reenters the atmosphere, uses its aerodynamic body flaps to control its descent, ignites its Raptor engines to maneuver into a vertical position, and carefully hovers near the tower. The giant chopstick arms then close around designated hard points under the upper structure of the rocket, securing the vehicle in mid-air before lower engine power completely cuts out.

By shifting the mechanical complexity and weight of landing gear from the vehicle to the stationary ground infrastructure, SpaceX solves two massive problems at once. First, it significantly increases payload capacity by eliminating dead weight on the spacecraft. Second, it unlocks rapid reusability. In conventional rocketry, a landed booster must be picked up from a landing pad, transported back to an assembly facility, inspected, and remounted onto a launch mount—a process taking weeks or months. Catching the rocket directly on the tower allows for immediate inspection, refueling, and stacked re-launch directly from the same structure, dramatically reducing turnaround time and operational cost. Performing this catch successfully on the Starship vehicle itself represents a major structural milestone towards making fully reusable, rapid space transport economically and technically viable.

Who's involved: SpaceX, Elon Musk, and Mechazilla.

## James Webb telescope finds ice older than the star it orbits

**Category:** Research  |  **Found via:** Ars Technica

Researchers using the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) have discovered widespread water and carbon dioxide ice within the debris disk surrounding Beta Pictoris, a young star located 64 light-years from Earth. By using Webb’s Near Infrared Spectrograph (NIRSpec) to measure starlight reflected across infrared wavelengths, scientists analyzed light absorption signatures to identify chemical compositions. They found that carbon dioxide ice was broadly distributed and physically trapped inside water ice, forming a mixed or dirty ice structure in proportions very similar to comets in our own Solar System.

This finding is novel because previous attempts to locate water ice around Beta Pictoris failed, leaving astronomers puzzled over how carbon monoxide gas continued to exist in the system despite being rapidly destroyed by stellar ultraviolet radiation. Theoretical models suggested pure ice grains would be destroyed by UV light quickly, but dirty ice could survive much longer. Crucially, the JWST observations revealed that the water ice is almost entirely amorphous—lacking a crystalline structure—indicating that it froze rapidly at temperatures below 100 Kelvin in deep space. This proves the ice originated in interstellar clouds long before the star Beta Pictoris and its surrounding planets formed, rather than freezing later within the stellar disk.

This discovery enables scientists to better understand planetary formation by confirming that primordial interstellar ice can survive the violent early stages of star creation. Because these icy grains are continually released through planetesimal collisions in the outer disk, the finding demonstrates that the raw, volatile ingredients necessary to form water-rich and potentially habitable planets may be standard across newly forming planetary systems throughout the galaxy.

Who's involved: Sarah Betti (Space Telescope Science Institute), James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) team, and Science journal.

## Winemakers are learning that conservation can be good for business

**Category:** Research  |  **Found via:** Ars Technica

This story covers a study by geography and sustainability researchers analyzing how vineyard operators in South Africa's Western Cape adapt to severe climate challenges. The region has experienced temperature increases rising far faster than global averages, leading to severe droughts and extreme storms. Researchers surveyed growers across 107 vineyards—roughly 20% of the area's total—to evaluate how sustainable land management practices are integrated into commercial grape production.

Traditionally, agricultural production and environmental conservation have been treated as opposing priorities, where setting aside land for native species or reducing chemical inputs was viewed as taking resources away from profit generation. The key novel finding of this research is that winegrowers increasingly view ecological conservation as a core business investment rather than a constraint. Facing rising costs for chemical fertilizers, pesticides, and water, vineyard managers are leveraging natural ecosystem services to build operational resilience.

Practices highlight how environmental balance directly supports yield and quality. Winemakers are planting diverse cover crops between vine rows to improve soil structure, reduce erosion, and enhance water retention during dry periods. Others are using livestock like cattle to naturally fertilize and aerate soil, or establishing habitats for natural predators to control pests without expensive chemical sprays. Furthermore, clearing invasive plant species along natural waterways helps protect local water tables. Participating in formal environmental initiatives, such as the Integrated Production of Wine certification or local wildlife programs, provides a double benefit: it improves ecological stability on the farm while offering a marketing advantage to eco-conscious consumers globally.

This finding enables researchers and agricultural managers worldwide to design integrated farming frameworks that treat local biomes as essential production infrastructure. By showing that biodiversity preservation directly mitigates supply chain risks caused by climate variability, the study provides a template for other drought-stressed agricultural sectors seeking to balance long-term financial viability with ecological health.

Who's involved: Researchers from the University of Tennessee, Michigan State University, University of Florida, and Southwestern University, studying vineyard practices in South Africa's Western Cape.

## Microsoft's school email bill gives rivals something to smile about

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** The Register

This story covers the ongoing fallout across the global education sector following Microsoft's announcement that it is ending its long-standing free email, cloud storage, and collaboration tier for students. For years, software vendors have offered free or deeply discounted enterprise cloud suites to schools. This strategy served as a classic acquisition model: by embedding tools like Outlook, Teams, and Word into everyday student life, technology companies secured long-term vendor lock-in as students entered the workforce already trained on their software ecosystem.

Under the new licensing structure, set to take effect for renewals on or after January 1, 2028, Microsoft is stripping core services like Exchange Online, SharePoint Online, and Teams from its baseline free tier (Microsoft 365 Education A0). To maintain basic email and collaboration tools, educational institutions will be forced to transition to paid tiers costing several dollars per student annually. While per-user fees sound low, they scale up to substantial recurring annual budget increases across large universities or school districts comprising tens of thousands of users.

This sudden shift in pricing dynamics creates significant strategic opportunities across the broader software industry. Competing vendors offering open-source or self-hosted platforms, such as Nextcloud, Collabora Productivity, and Element, are pitching their solutions as cost-effective alternatives. Beyond financial cost, the situation highlights growing concerns around vendor lock-in and digital sovereignty, particularly in European countries like Germany and Switzerland. Government bodies and educational institutions are re-evaluating their reliance on dominant proprietary cloud ecosystems, recognizing that vendor-controlled price hikes can create systemic budgetary risks.

The implications for the tech industry are two-fold: educational IT departments are actively forced to weigh the high labor cost of migrating legacy data against the recurring expense of paid Microsoft licenses, while open-source software providers gain a unique commercial window to capture public sector market share.

Who's involved: Microsoft, Nextcloud, Collabora Productivity, Element, and the UK Department for Education.
