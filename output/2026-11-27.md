## AWS adds virtual iPhones to its cloud

**Category:** Technical Release  |  **Found via:** The Register

Amazon Web Services (AWS) has expanded its AWS Device Farm testing environment by introducing virtual mobile devices for both iOS and Android alongside its existing physical device hardware. In software development, testing applications on physical mobile hardware can be bottlenecked by device availability, provision times, and physical management constraints. Virtualized mobile devices solve this by emulating mobile operating systems inside scalable cloud instances, allowing virtual phones to spin up in seconds to execute automated software tests.

Underneath the hood, AWS utilizes software virtualization to run emulated operating system instances—specifically supporting Google Pixel devices running Android and virtual iPhones running iOS. This architecture enables developers to integrate rapid, high-volume automated testing directly into early Continuous Integration and Continuous Deployment (CI/CD) pipelines. Software teams can trigger automated virtual device runs for rapid operational checks during code check-ins and pull requests, saving real hardware testing for later integration stages. Developers can interact with these virtualized environments using the exact same APIs and automation frameworks they already use for physical hardware in AWS Device Farm.

While this mechanism provides significant speed and scaling advantages, the initial rollout offers limited software and hardware choices. The available catalog features selective configurations like Google Pixel 10 devices on Android and iPhone 16 devices on iOS, leaving out popular high-end hardware options and foldable device form factors. Nevertheless, virtual device cloud hosting streamlines software deployment by shifting automated software verification earlier into development workflows.

Who's involved: Amazon Web Services (AWS), AWS Device Farm, Apple (iOS/iPhone), and Google (Android/Pixel).

## Netflix’s coziest show has made a very quiet return

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** The Verge

The entertainment streaming industry has reached a scale where massive platform growth is actively altering how media is discovered and consumed. As giant subscription video-on-demand services expand their offerings into reality programming, gaming, and live sports broadcasts, their content catalogs have become intensely saturated. This expansion introduces significant operational friction: the algorithmic and promotional discovery systems designed to serve viewers can easily fail to surface niche, high-quality programming, even to users who have an established history of consuming similar material.

This dynamic is clearly illustrated by the return of Midnight Diner: Tokyo Stories for a third season on Netflix after a seven-year production hiatus. Despite strong critical appeal and a loyal following from its original broadcast run on Japanese television and subsequent streaming seasons, the show's return arrived with minimal promotional push, primarily appearing only on regional marketing channels. Compounding this shift, the series departed from the classic binge-release model that built the streaming era, opting instead for a staggered, weekly episodic schedule that mirrors traditional television broadcast habits.

For the media industry, this scenario highlights two critical trends. First, platforms are increasingly balancing all-at-once releases with serialized weekly drops to prolong subscriber engagement, reduce churn, and retain users over multiple billing cycles. Second, it exposes the vulnerabilities of digital content discovery in mega-catalogs. As streaming libraries balloon, high-value cultural productions risk being buried underneath high-priority tentpole properties and live events unless companies overhaul how recommendation engines surface quieter, specialized releases to dedicated audiences.

Who's involved: Netflix, actor Kaoru Kobayashi, and The Verge.

## Nvidia hits pause on chip shipments to Southeast Asia

**Category:** Policy / Security  |  **Found via:** TechCrunch

United States export control policy has expanded its focus from restricting the direct physical shipment of high-end artificial intelligence silicon to closing remote access avenues that bypass international trade barriers. To restrict China's advancement in cutting-edge AI and defense capabilities, the U.S. government previously blocked direct sales of advanced graphics processing units (GPUs) to Chinese entities. However, Chinese enterprises countered these trade barriers by utilizing remote computing arrangements: instead of importing physical chips into mainland China, companies leased compute capacity hosted in data centers across third-party nations, effectively accessing restricted processing power over the cloud.

In response to this workaround, the U.S. Department of Commerce tightened regulatory enforcement to prevent foreign data hubs from serving as computing proxies. The policy crackdown directly impacts the broader global supply chain, leading Nvidia to instruct key hardware assembly and manufacturing partners, including Dell and Super Micro Computer, to temporarily halt AI server deliveries destined for Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Indonesia. These four Southeast Asian countries have recently evolved into major regional hosting hubs, with research estimates indicating that third-party facilities in these areas have provided roughly a quarter of China's remote AI computational bandwidth.

Moving forward, hardware manufacturers and cloud infrastructure providers must implement rigorous compliance screening and "know your customer" verifications to ensure data center tenants are not foreign-restricted end users. While Nvidia and its hardware partners assess these regional compliance standards, regional cloud infrastructure expansions will face operational disruptions, administrative delays, and closer auditing by international trade authorities, potentially prompting diplomatic and trade tensions across the Indo-Pacific tech corridor.

Who's involved: Nvidia, Dell, Super Micro Computer, the U.S. Department of Commerce, and the Institute for Progress.

**Source:** https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/26/nvidia-hits-pause-on-chip-shipments-to-southeast-asia/

*The pause on shipments to Southeast Asia is based on reported claims from The Wall Street Journal, and the figure that remote data centers supply about 24% of China's AI compute power is an outside estimate by the Institute for Progress.*

## X finally gets parental controls after 20 years

**Category:** Policy / Security  |  **Found via:** TechCrunch

Social media platform X (formerly Twitter) has launched its first comprehensive parental control system after two decades of operation. This change introduces a native framework for parents and guardians to monitor and manage how teenage users interact with content on the platform.

Under the new system, accounts registered to teenagers aged 13 to 17 will be placed into a "protected mode" by default. This baseline setting automatically blocks explicit or sensitive media and turns off targeted advertising. Parents who link their accounts to their teen's account can configure customized safety boundaries, such as setting daily usage time limits, requiring parental approval for new followers, restricting feed recommendations strictly to accounts the teen already follows, filtering out specific keywords, and specifying who is allowed to send direct messages.

What makes X's implementation distinct from platforms like Meta or TikTok is its explicit emphasis on teen transparency. Teenagers retain visibility into their account settings and receive notifications explaining any restrictions applied by their parents. Furthermore, to preserve user privacy, the controls do not grant parents the ability to read private messages, view search histories, or post on behalf of the teenager.

This policy release comes in direct response to intense regulatory and public pressure surrounding child safety on the network. Prior to this update, X faced widespread criticism for inadequate safeguards regarding adult content, misinformation, and AI-generated explicit media generated by its integrated chatbot, Grok. While X historically catered to an adult demographic, industry research indicated that a significant portion of younger users access the platform, making formal safety protections necessary to navigate tightening global compliance standards for minor protection online.

Who's involved: X (formerly Twitter), Elon Musk, and Michael O'Herlihy (Head of Safety at X).

## Ford unveils the GT41 as it hopes to win Le Mans again

**Category:** Technical Release  |  **Found via:** Ars Technica

Ford has officially unveiled the GT41, a high-performance endurance race car engineered to compete in the World Endurance Championship (WEC) Hypercar class and the 24 Hours of Le Mans. To solve the challenge of building a competitive, cost-effective endurance racer, Ford adopted the LMDh (Le Mans Daytona h) technical specification. Rather than engineering every component from scratch, the GT41 utilizes a standardized carbon-fiber chassis developed in partnership with French race car constructor Oreca, alongside a standardized hybrid system and transmission. Ford differentiates the vehicle by integrating its own custom 5.4-liter V8 engine—derived from the Mustang's Coyote engine—along with proprietary software control units to balance output between the internal combustion engine and the hybrid system. Furthermore, the design leverages aerodynamic regulations that allow manufacturers to incorporate styling cues from production road cars while adhering to strict downforce and drag caps.

Who's involved: Ford Racing (Dan Sayers), Oreca, Zack Snyder, and Matt Damon.
