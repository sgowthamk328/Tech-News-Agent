## Jackery made its portable power stations even smaller

**Category:** Technical Release  |  **Found via:** The Verge

Jackery has launched the Explorer 100D, its smallest portable power station to date, designed to pack high-capacity charging into a flight-friendly size. The core technological upgrade in this release is the transition to Lithium Iron Phosphate (LiFePO4 or LFP) battery chemistry inside a compact form factor. Traditional portable power banks commonly rely on standard lithium-ion chemistries (such as NMC), which offer high energy density but suffer from faster degradation over time and higher thermal risk. LFP chemistry replaces nickel and cobalt with iron and phosphate, providing superior thermal and chemical stability, significantly improved fire safety, and a much longer lifespan. While standard lithium-ion batteries typically last between 500 and 1,000 charge cycles before their capacity degrades noticeably, LFP cells can endure up to 3,000 full charge-discharge cycles while retaining most of their health, equating to roughly eight years of regular use.

The main problem this product solves is balancing long-term battery longevity and high power delivery with stringent physical size and airline travel constraints. By capping the capacity at 96 watt-hours (Wh), Jackery keeps the device just under the strict 100Wh limit enforced by the FAA and international aviation authorities for lithium batteries carried in airplane cabins. Despite its small size, the internal power delivery architecture supports up to 140W fast-charging over USB-C ports, allowing users to charge power-hungry devices like laptops alongside smaller accessories via a secondary USB-A port. Additionally, the unit incorporates dedicated DC charging circuitry capable of accepting up to 100W of direct solar panel input, allowing off-grid recharging in about 1.5 hours without needing an intermediate AC adapter.

Who's involved: Jackery, Explorer 100D.

## India floats AI plan under which ‘The model travels to the data, rather than the data travelling to the model’

**Category:** Policy / Security  |  **Found via:** The Register

India's Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) has introduced a national framework called the Data Empowerment and Protection Architecture (DEPA) for AI Training. This initiative changes the paradigm of artificial intelligence development by introducing a decentralized training architecture summarized by the principle: "the model travels to the data, rather than the data travelling to the model." Traditionally, AI developers collect massive datasets from individuals, institutions, and the web, centralizing them on private servers to train large language models. This practice presents major data privacy risks, intellectual property concerns, and loss of local control over sovereign data.

Under DEPA, data providers—such as hospitals, banks, and government institutions—do not hand over raw files to AI developers. Instead, encrypted and anonymized data is hosted within secure, hardware-isolated environments operated by centralized Data Exchange Platforms (DEPs). Developers, designated as Training Data Consumers, send their AI model algorithms into these secure environments to execute training directly on-site. The framework applies differential privacy techniques to prevent models from memorizing or outputting sensitive individual details, while immutable audit logs track all activity.

This policy directly affects AI developers, technology firms, and domestic institutions across India's healthcare, financial, and educational sectors. Going forward, multinational Big Tech firms and local developers seeking to leverage Indian institutional data must comply with these restricted execution environments and access rules set by data owners. By establishing DEPA as part of its Digital Public Infrastructure, India aims to build domestically tailored AI tools, secure financial inclusion for underserved populations, and project a international model for sovereign, privacy-preserving AI governance.

Who's involved: Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY), Government of India, Professor Ajay Kumar Sood.

## Google says Asian advertisers spending less due to Europe's customs changes

**Category:** Policy / Security  |  **Found via:** The Register

The European Union has updated its cross-border commerce regulations by eliminating the long-standing 'de minimis' customs duty exemption on low-value imports. Under the previous regulatory framework, international packages valued below €150 entered the European market free of import duties. The EU implemented a new structure that applies a flat €3 customs duty to every consignment valued under €150 originating from non-EU countries. This policy shift was designed to level the playing field for European domestic retailers who previously faced price undercutting from tax-exempt international shipments.

This regulatory alteration primarily impacts major Asian e-commerce conglomerates—most notably Chinese platforms such as Shein, Temu, and Alibaba—that rely heavily on shipping small, direct-to-consumer packages into Europe. Because these e-tailers generate substantial sales through digital marketing, the added import costs have compressed their operational margins. Consequently, these companies have scaled back their marketing expenditures on major digital advertising platforms. Alphabet (Google) confirmed during its quarterly earnings call that this specific EU policy shift directly contributed to a noticeable deceleration in ad revenue growth from its Asia-Pacific advertising clients.

Looking ahead, this policy move signals a tightening global trade environment for low-cost international e-commerce. As European authorities enforce these import fees to protect local economies, other jurisdictions are likely to inspect similar tax loopholes. E-commerce platforms will need to adjust their logistics and pricing strategies, while global ad networks like Google and Meta must prepare for reduced ad spend from overseas retailers who can no longer rely on duty-free cross-border shipping models.

Who's involved: European Union, Google (Alphabet), Meta, Temu, Shein, and Alibaba.

## Linus Torvalds says Linux kernel is out of ‘stabilization phase’ and using ‘much healthier model’ for AI contributions

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** The Register

The development cycle of the Linux kernel represents the backbone of global computing infrastructure, running everything from cloud servers and supercomputers to Android smartphones. In modern software engineering, artificial intelligence tools have increasingly been deployed to scan codebases, spot subtle bugs, and generate automated code fixes. However, introducing automated suggestions into a project as sensitive and complex as the operating system kernel presents severe operational challenges for maintainers, who must vet every line for correctness and security.

Historically, kernel development follows a structured rhythm: a two-week "merge window" where new features and major changes are pulled into the codebase, followed by several weeks of "release candidates" focused exclusively on stabilization and bug fixing. Initially, an influx of AI-generated bug reports and code patches flooded this later stabilization phase, overwhelming maintainers with late-stage churn and low-quality automated fixes. Linus Torvalds, the creator and primary maintainer of Linux, has noted a major turning point with the release candidate for version 7.4. Rather than disrupting the stabilization period, AI-assisted fixes and issue discoveries have shifted into the standard merge window, signaling that automated contributions are integrating into ordinary engineering workflows rather than creating ad-hoc fire drills.

This shift carries significant implications for the broader software industry. It demonstrates that large open-source projects can successfully adapt to machine-generated contributions without compromising rigorous review standards. Torvalds observed that while AI frequently uncovers legitimate underlying issues, human maintainers still routinely rewrite the proposed code patches before final inclusion. This dynamic establishes AI as an automated discovery tool and drafting assistant rather than an autonomous committer. As version 7.4 approaches a potential Long-Term Support designation, its massive volume of roughly 20,000 commits illustrates how AI tooling is expanding code throughput across enterprise hardware ecosystems—including AMD, Apple, and Google platforms—while keeping human oversight firmly at the center of critical infrastructure maintenance.

Who's involved: Linus Torvalds, the Linux kernel community, AMD, Apple, Google, and Qualcomm.

## NASA hopes the commercial space industry can help save Hubble

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** The Verge

Since its launch in 1990, the Hubble Space Telescope has orbited Earth in low Earth orbit, where atmospheric drag gradually bleeds off its orbital energy. Over decades, this drag causes an orbiting spacecraft to lose altitude, meaning Hubble will eventually enter denser layers of the atmosphere and crash back to Earth by the early to mid-2030s unless an external force boosts its orbit. Historically, NASA relied on the Space Shuttle fleet to conduct crewed servicing missions that raised Hubble's altitude and replaced degraded instruments. With the Space Shuttle retired since 2011, NASA no longer possesses its own dedicated vehicle capable of rendezvous and orbital reboost missions of this scale. Instead of retiring the observatory or funding a bespoke government mission, NASA has turned to the private space industry. The agency issued an open Request for Information seeking commercial proposals to rendezvous with Hubble and push it into a higher, more stable orbit. This move reflects a broader paradigm shift across the space economy: government agencies increasingly view private launch and servicing providers as cost-effective contractors for complex orbital logistics rather than designing proprietary public solutions from scratch. If commercial operators prove they can safely perform on-orbit servicing and reboosting on an aging, delicate asset like Hubble, it will validate a burgeoning market for commercial satellite life extension, debris removal, and on-orbit maintenance. Success would extend the operational lifespan of one of humanity's most important astronomical instruments while proving that commercial space infrastructure has matured enough to preserve legacy public science investments without public agencies footing the entire engineering pipeline. Who's involved: NASA, Administrator Jared Isaacman, and prospective commercial space servicing providers.

## Flock keeps ignoring requests to cancel its contracts

**Category:** Policy / Security  |  **Found via:** The Verge

Multiple local governments across the United States are facing unexpected obstacles when attempting to terminate their service contracts with Flock Safety, a prominent provider of automated license plate recognition (ALPR) systems. Across municipalities such as Plymouth and Dayton in Minnesota, as well as educational institutions like Elgin Community College in Illinois, officials have formally voted to exit their agreements only to encounter prolonged silence, delays, or outright non-compliance from the vendor. In some instances, surveillance hardware remained active weeks after termination notices, prompting local law enforcement officials—such as Plymouth's police chief—to physically disconnect device power cables to prevent unauthorized vehicle tracking. In Dayton, maintenance crews even reconnected deactivated units despite explicit municipal instructions to halt operations.

This dispute directly affects local municipalities, law enforcement agencies, and the general public whose vehicular movements are captured by these networks. When private surveillance hardware remains operational without active municipal authorization, serious legal and civil liberties concerns emerge regarding unauthorized data collection, chain of custody, and Fourth Amendment privacy protections. Because ALPR networks record timestamps, vehicle features, and location data that are often cross-referenced across broader law enforcement databases, continued passive collection without a valid contract undermines local governance and oversight.

Moving forward, these incidents are likely to prompt local governments to insert much stricter offboarding protocols, escrow clauses, and financial penalties into public safety technology contracts. Municipalities will demand legally binding service-level agreements governing the rapid decommissioning and removal of surveillance infrastructure upon contract termination. Additionally, these operational failures provide ammunition to privacy advocates and civil rights groups pushing for heightened regulatory constraints on automated public surveillance and public-private data-sharing networks.

Who's involved: Flock Safety, Plymouth Police Department (Chief Eric Fadden), the City of Dayton (Minnesota), and Elgin Community College.

**Source:** https://www.theverge.com/tech/1024232/flock-ignoring-cancel-contracts

*Flock spokesperson Andrew Smith acknowledged the removal request in Plymouth, but the company has not provided a public explanation for why unauthorized camera reactivations occurred in Dayton.*
