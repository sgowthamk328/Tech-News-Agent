## Boox’s tiny Picco e-reader should land in November

**Category:** Technical Release  |  **Found via:** The Verge

Electronic paper (e-paper) devices have long forced users into a design compromise between software flexibility and physical portability. Larger e-readers frequently adopt full mobile operating systems like Android, granting access to third-party reading applications but requiring powerful processors, large RAM allocations, and heavy battery packs. Conversely, ultra-compact, pocket-sized e-readers historically feature stripped-down hardware with rigid user interfaces, relying solely on physical buttons for navigation and restrictive proprietary transfer protocols. The Boox Picco resolves these engineering constraints through a specialized hardware and software architecture built for micro-sized reading.

Architecturally, the Picco centers around a 3.97-inch capacitive touchscreen e-paper display paired with multi-function physical buttons that can be assigned distinct actions for short and long presses. Instead of running Boox's typical Android software stack, the device uses a lightweight, stripped-down Linux operating system. This architectural pivot eliminates resource-heavy background services, reducing RAM overhead and power consumption while maintaining core micro-apps such as a timer, a task list, and a reading tracker. Hardware design is further optimized by removing onboard flash memory entirely; instead, the device relies on a microSD card slot for primary storage. To ensure seamless content management, the system integrates modern connectivity pipelines, enabling users to transfer documents via USB-C, local Wi-Fi via a companion app, or direct memory card loading.

This architecture solves the core problem of micro-e-reader usability. Previous devices in this ultra-portable category required awkward multi-button presses to navigate book libraries or lacked standard physical connections. By combining a touch-enabled e-paper panel with flexible local syncing and a low-overhead Linux environment, the Picco delivers a fluid, pocketable reading experience that operates efficiently without sacrificing modern file transfer conveniences or forcing users into closed ecosystems.

Who's involved: Onyx Boox, Xteink, and the IFA trade show.

## Research carried out using NetBSD

**Category:** Research  |  **Found via:** Hacker News
> Discovered via Hacker News; explanation is based on the verified source below, not the discovery post itself.

Operating systems serve as the foundational software layer coordinating hardware resources, memory allocation, and networking protocols. The NetBSD project maintains a gallery of research initiatives demonstrating how its highly clean, portable Unix codebase functions as an ideal experimental substrate for academic and industrial computer science research. Rather than focusing on a single study, this research compilation highlights systemic findings across virtual memory design, high-speed networking, real-time multimedia storage, and distributed open-source collaboration.

The primary novel finding demonstrated across these projects is that a clean, highly modular operating system kernel allows researchers to re-engineer core subsystems without introducing performance bottlenecks or architectural instability. For example, the implementation of the UVM virtual memory system proved that replacing legacy BSD memory abstractions with flexible data movement mechanisms significantly improves memory management performance during process creation and pageouts. Similarly, network research conducted using NetBSD demonstrated that optimized TCP stack architectures could overcome severe latency and bandwidth limitations, as evidenced by NASA’s experiments with satellite TCP protocol extensions and SUNET’s record-breaking Internet2 data transfer that achieved throughput speeds of over 69 petabit-meters per second using a single TCP stream.

These technical breakthroughs are novel because they challenge the assumption that high-performance kernel modifications require dedicated, single-purpose operating systems. By utilizing NetBSD’s portable framework, researchers proved that advanced features—such as zero-copy multimedia buffer management (mmbuf) or constant-time kernel timer callouts—could be integrated directly into a standard Unix environment across diverse hardware architectures, from space-constrained embedded systems to high-speed backbone routers.

Looking forward, these research outcomes enable the next generation of resilient systems engineering. The networking extensions pioneered on NetBSD directly informed standard IPv6 and IPsec protocol implementations (such as the KAME project), while its lightweight runtime footprint enabled NASA to deploy distributed microgravity monitoring sensors on the International Space Station. Furthermore, these benchmark designs continue to inform modern operating system development, high-speed routing hardware, and real-time fault-tolerant edge computing.

**Category:** Policy / Security  |  **Found via:** TechCrunch

To understand the recent regulatory developments surrounding Tesla's Cybercab, it is essential to look at how automobile safety is governed in the United States. Traditionally, federal safety standards require all road-going motor vehicles to feature human operational controls, such as steering wheels, accelerator pedals, and brake pedals. When Tesla began deploying its custom Cybercab—a fully autonomous vehicle built without traditional manual controls—on public streets in Austin, Texas, it relied on a process called self-certification. Under this framework, vehicle manufacturers independently attest that their designs satisfy all applicable federal safety standards prior to commercial release.

What changed in this situation is that the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) opened a formal safety investigation into Tesla just hours after the first Cybercabs entered public roadways. NHTSA's intervention targets the potential safety risks and regulatory non-compliance of deploying driverless vehicles that completely lack manual override capabilities under existing motor vehicle statutes.

This regulatory action directly affects Tesla, which has anchored its valuation and long-term product strategy around autonomous mobility. It also impacts the broader autonomous vehicle industry—including competitors like Waymo and Zoox—by signaling how strictly federal authorities will enforce safety compliance for purpose-built driverless hardware. Furthermore, municipal authorities, urban transit planners, and everyday road users are affected as federal oversight shapes the timeline and safety assurances of public autonomous transportation.

Moving forward, several critical developments will unfold. NHTSA will evaluate whether Tesla's deployment violates safety statutes or presents an unreasonable safety risk, which could lead to mandatory operational restrictions or fines. Concurrently, the U.S. Department of Transportation has proposed updated administrative rules that would formally eliminate manual control requirements for purpose-built autonomous vehicles. If these rules are finalized, automakers will gain a clearer legal pathway to manufacture pedal-free vehicles nationwide. In the interim, Tesla must demonstrate that its autonomous driving software can maintain safe operations without human intervention under strict federal scrutiny.

## Cultivating Trust

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** Hacker News
> Discovered via Hacker News; explanation is based on the verified source below, not the discovery post itself.

The rapid proliferation of artificial intelligence coding agents across software engineering has fundamentally altered how digital products are built. Modern AI tools can instantly produce vast volumes of application code, enabling developers to prototype features and resolve tasks far faster than traditional manual programming allowed. However, this surge in speed has introduced a major operational challenge across the tech industry: code entropy. Because AI agents generate code effortlessly, corporate software repositories are experiencing an explosive growth in total line count and pull requests. This dramatic increase creates severe code review bottlenecks, introduces subtle bugs, and degrades long-term software maintainability, raising fundamental questions about engineer trust and code quality.

The context surrounding this shift highlights a growing consensus among engineering leaders that generating code is no longer the primary bottleneck in software creation; managing software quality and system trust is. When engineers rely heavily on AI tools without deep comprehension, technical debt accumulates rapidly. To mitigate these risks, engineering teams are adopting structured operational strategies designed to preserve code integrity without sacrificing the speed advantages brought by automation.

The implications for the technology industry are significant. Engineering organizations are shifting their focus toward deterministic tooling—such as automated linters, static type checkers, and continuous integration pipelines—to act as automated gatekeepers against low-quality code. Teams are also enforcing policies requiring smaller, more reviewable code changes, and insisting that human engineers explicitly author test scenarios to define business logic before allowing AI agents to implement solutions. Ultimately, developer roles are evolving from writing line-by-line syntax to acting as system architects and critical evaluators who remain personally accountable for what reaches production.

For the broader market, companies that establish clear engineering guidelines and rigorous verification processes will successfully scale their output using AI automation. Conversely, organizations that fail to cultivate engineering accountability and robust testing infrastructures risk deteriorating codebase stability, rising operational costs, and frequent production outages as unverified AI code expands unchecked.

## NetBSD 9.5 released and EOL for NetBSD-9

**Category:** Technical Release  |  **Found via:** Hacker News
> Discovered via Hacker News; explanation is based on the verified source below, not the discovery post itself.

Operating systems rely on structured release cycles to balance continuous software innovation with daily operational stability. NetBSD is a Unix-like open-source operating system renowned for its extreme portability across diverse computer hardware architectures, ranging from vintage systems and lightweight embedded microcontrollers to high-performance enterprise servers. To keep production environments reliable, operating system maintainers divide software development into main active branches and stable release branches. Stable branches receive continuous security hardening and bug fixes while strictly avoiding disruptive architectural modifications or altered software interfaces.

The release of NetBSD 9.5 serves as the fifth and final maintenance update to the NetBSD 9 stable branch, which was originally established to provide long-term platform consistency. Operating system deployment creates a persistent operational challenge for system administrators: maintaining active defense against newly discovered security vulnerabilities and system crashes without risking downtime caused by major updates. NetBSD 9.5 addresses this problem by carefully backporting a curated subset of critical security patches and stability fixes accumulated since the previous release, NetBSD 9.4, while preserving complete Application Binary Interface (ABI) compatibility with NetBSD 9.0. This design guarantees that existing third-party applications, compiled binaries, and system configurations continue to function seamlessly without requiring reinstallation or recompilation.

Alongside this release, the NetBSD Project announced the formal End-of-Life (EOL) designation for the entire NetBSD 9.x series. In software engineering lifecycle management, an EOL milestone indicates that a software branch has reached the end of its official support window and will no longer receive security advisories, bug fixes, or maintenance support. Releasing NetBSD 9.5 provides a final, fully patched baseline for existing deployments, while signaling to system administrators that they must plan migrations to active supported release branches—such as NetBSD 10.2 or NetBSD 11.0—to ensure continued security against emerging threats. This routine sunset mechanism allows the open-source developer community to consolidate technical resources toward supporting modern hardware drivers and updated kernel subsystems.

Who's involved: The NetBSD Project, The NetBSD Foundation, Martin Husemann, and the NetBSD operating system.

## German company becomes first in Europe to launch fully commercial orbital rocket

**Category:** Industry News  |  **Found via:** Ars Technica

The European space sector has long relied on heavily subsidized, state-backed aerospace conglomerates like Arianespace and Avio to secure access to orbit. Under the traditional European procurement model, known as geographical return, government contracts were distributed among member states based on public financial contributions rather than free-market competition. This slow, top-down structure caused Europe to fall significantly behind private Western competitors, most notably SpaceX, forcing European governments and satellite operators to pay premium prices or rely on foreign launch providers for orbital transport.

German startup Isar Aerospace has disrupted this paradigm by becoming the first private European venture to successfully launch a commercially developed, two-stage rocket named Spectrum into low-Earth orbit. Founded in 2018 by university students and backed by roughly $1 billion in private venture capital alongside recent European Space Agency contract awards, Isar designed Spectrum to transport medium-sized payloads up to one metric ton. Lifting off from the Arctic Andøya Spaceport in Norway, the flight demonstrated that private European firms can rapidly develop orbital launch hardware outside traditional government-led programs.

This milestone carries profound implications for the global space economy and European geopolitical strategy. Strategically, sovereign orbital access allows European institutions to deploy defense, communications, and scientific satellites without dependence on American or other international commercial operators. Operationally, Isar's accomplishment signals a shift toward a venture-backed "NewSpace" ecosystem within Europe, encouraging regional launch competition alongside emerging startups like Rocket Factory Augsburg and PLD Space.

If Isar successfully transitions from this initial orbital test to scalable mass production at its Bavarian facility, it could significantly drive down launch costs across Europe. Furthermore, this success validates a modern procurement approach for the European Space Agency, which is increasingly adopting competitive, outcome-based funding models similar to NASA's commercial launch initiatives.

Who's involved: Isar Aerospace, chief executive officer Daniel Metzler, the European Space Agency (ESA), and Andøya Spaceport.

## Farmed salmon may not be as nutritious as it once was, new research suggests

**Category:** Research  |  **Found via:** Ars Technica

For decades, public health organizations like the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) and the American Heart Association have recommended eating fatty fish such as salmon at least twice a week. These guidelines are based on the premise that salmon is a prime source of essential Omega-3 fatty acids, which support cardiovascular health. However, recent scientific research led by USDA researchers reveals a significant decline in the nutritional quality of farmed salmon, specifically a marked reduction in these beneficial Omega-3 levels.

The primary driver behind this nutritional shift is a radical change in what farmed salmon are fed. Naturally, salmon are carnivores that rely on smaller wild fish for sustenance. As global aquaculture expanded rapidly to meet rising seafood demand, feeding captive populations with wild-caught fish led to severe marine overexploitation. To make aquaculture more sustainable for ocean ecosystems, feed manufacturers systematically replaced fishmeal and fish oils with terrestrial crops, notably soy and canola. In major producing nations like Norway, marine ingredients in salmon feed dropped from 90 percent in 1990 to roughly 23 percent by 2020.

This finding is novel because it demonstrates an unintended trade-off in sustainable agriculture: solving marine depletion by switching to land-based crops has diluted the precise health benefits that drove consumer demand in the first place. Furthermore, expanding terrestrial soy farming to feed fish has displaced rainforests and increased carbon emissions, trading ocean exploitation for land degradation.

This research enables several critical next steps across public policy and agricultural science. First, it highlights that official nutritional databases and dietary guidelines rely on outdated nutritional profiles, signaling a need for broader, nationally representative seafood audits. Second, it accelerates the demand for novel, truly sustainable feed ingredients—such as lab-grown microalgae oils or insect meal—that can restore high Omega-3 concentrations in farmed fish without reliance on deforested land or overfished oceans.

Who's involved: USDA scientists, Laura Lee Cascada (Aquaculture Accountability Project), Matthew Hayek (New York University), and global aquaculture feed producers.

**Source:** https://arstechnica.com/science/2026/09/farmed-salmon-may-not-be-as-nutritious-as-it-once-was-new-research-suggests/

*While the nutritional shift is supported by feed data, the study authors note their sample size was not large enough to mandate an immediate official update to federal dietary advice.*

## Time lords prepare to kick leap seconds into the next millennium

**Category:** Policy / Security  |  **Found via:** The Register

The General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM) has introduced a draft resolution to effectively eliminate leap seconds starting May 20, 2027. Under this proposed policy, Coordinated Universal Time (UTC)—which is maintained by hyper-precise atomic clocks—will no longer be forcibly synchronized with UT1, the time standard tied to Earth's variable rotation. Instead, UTC will be allowed to continuously run, permitting a drift between atomic time and astronomical time of up to one hour over the next millennium.

This policy change impacts global digital infrastructure, including telecommunication networks, financial trading systems, electrical power grids, cloud data centers, and satellite navigation networks. Historically, leap seconds were added periodically to keep atomic time within 0.9 seconds of Earth's rotation. However, because modern network servers and software architectures were hardcoded under the assumption that leap seconds are strictly additive, recent fluctuations in Earth's rotation speed introduced a 30 percent chance of needing a negative leap second (subtracting a second) by 2035. Subtracting a second poses catastrophic risks of software crashes, timing unsynchronization, and systemic outages across critical national infrastructure.

What happens next is a formal vote by national delegates from participating countries at the upcoming CGPM meeting. Because the majority of participating nations favor mitigating infrastructural risks, the resolution is widely expected to pass. Once enacted in May 2027, system administrators and network engineers will no longer need to manage complex leap-second adjustments or prepare for unprecedented negative leap seconds, ensuring decades of operational stability for time-sensitive global networks. Over the coming centuries, scientific bodies may eventually re-evaluate how to handle cumulative drift, but for the foreseeable future, the operational risks of leap seconds will be effectively neutralized.

Who's involved: General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM), Setnam Shemar (National Physical Laboratory), and international metrology delegates.
